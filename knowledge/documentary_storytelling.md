# Documentary storytelling — business & legal stories

Working knowledge for researcher, writer, critic, scout. Complements `channel/storytelling.md` (the channel formula); this file is the craft.

## 1. A story is a person wanting something against resistance
- Companies are not characters; **people inside them are**. Find the founder, the executive who signed, the whistleblower, the victim, the prosecutor, the judge, the small creator. Give the viewer someone to follow.
- For each main character know: what they wanted, what they risked, what they did, what it cost them, and (if sourced) what they said.
- Antagonists do not need to be villains — a force (an algorithm, a contract clause, market pressure) can be the antagonist. Never manufacture a villain the record does not support.

## 2. Structure
- **Spine:** status quo → disruption → escalating attempts and complications → crisis/turn → consequence → what it means now.
- **Three acts in 18 minutes:** Act 1 (0–4 min) the world and the problem; Act 2 (4–14 min) escalation with at least two reversals; Act 3 (14–18 min) the reckoning, the cost, the open question.
- **Chronology vs narrative:** tell it in the order that maximizes tension, but never imply causation that the record does not show. Time jumps need explicit dates on screen.
- **Reveal architecture:** decide in advance which facts are withheld and where they land. The best document/number is a reveal, not background.
- **Dramatic irony:** tell the viewer something the characters did not know yet ("At the time, nobody at the company had read paragraph 12.").
- **Foreshadow the ending** in the first minute without giving it away.

## 3. Scene vs summary
- A **scene** is a specific moment: date, place, who, what was said or signed. Scenes create emotion and memory.
- A **summary** moves time forward quickly. Use summary to get between scenes; build the film on scenes.
- Research should therefore collect moments: a hearing, a filing date, an email quoted in a complaint, a board vote, a phone call described in an indictment, a sentencing.

## 4. Specificity is credibility
- Exact numbers with their meaning, exact dates, exact titles of documents, exact quotes.
- One vivid, verified detail beats five general statements (the color of the car in the indictment, the name of the fake artist, the count of songs, the time stamp of the message).
- Humanize numbers: convert to something the viewer can feel (per day, per song, per employee, compared to a known amount) — only if the arithmetic is simple and stated as our illustration, never as a claim a party made.

## 5. Explaining mechanisms
- Explain the **minimum** mechanism needed for the next beat, at the moment it is needed.
- Use one concrete example before the rule ("Imagine one account uploads 12 songs a day…" → then the rule).
- Analogies must be accurate; flag when an analogy simplifies.
- Legal terms: say the plain-English meaning first, then the term once ("an agreement not to prosecute if the company behaves — a deferred prosecution agreement").

## 6. Fairness makes it stronger
- Steelman every side. The strongest version of the defense makes the story more credible and protects the channel.
- Denials go right next to the allegations they answer, not at the end.
- Uncertainty is a story tool: "What the court has not decided yet is…" keeps viewers for the ending.

## 7. What makes a topic a strong film (for scouts and researchers)
- Recognizable surface + hidden mechanism + a person with stakes + a concrete, already-materialized cost + a document trail + a reversal.
- Weak signals: only one event (a lawsuit just filed), only companies (no people), only secondary reporting, no ending, the angle every competitor already used.
- The best angle is often **one level up or one level down** from the headline: the industry pattern the case reveals (up), or the single person inside it (down).

## 8. Narration voice (WHAT IT COST)
- Calm, precise, confident; the narrator knows the documents, not the gossip.
- Short declarative sentences; no rhetorical-question chains; at most one per section.
- Attribution woven naturally ("according to the indictment", "the complaint claims", "the company says").
- No moralizing; let the consequence speak.
- Last line of the film returns to the title question: what it cost.

## 9. Common failures
- Starting with background instead of the story.
- Treating the lawsuit as the story instead of what the lawsuit reveals.
- Too many names — keep 3–5 named characters; label the rest by role.
- Ending on "we'll see what happens" without saying what would decide it and why it matters.
- Explaining the law for longer than 45 s at a time.
