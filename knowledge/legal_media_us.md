# US media law & YouTube policy — working knowledge

For researcher, writer, critic, defamation-risk, final-check, packaging. This is editorial risk guidance, not legal advice. `channel/legal.md` (status vocabulary) stays binding; this file explains *why*.

## 1. Defamation — the basics
A false **statement of fact**, about an **identifiable** person or company, **published** to others, that **harms reputation**, made with the required **fault**.
- **Truth is a complete defense**, and "substantial truth" is enough — but only for what the record actually establishes. Calling someone a fraudster when they were only sued for fraud is not substantially true.
- **Public figures and public officials** (CEOs of famous companies, public companies, politicians) must prove **actual malice** — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth. Reckless disregard includes ignoring obvious contrary evidence, relying on a source you had reason to doubt, or inventing details. Our sourcing discipline is the defense.
- **Private figures** (small creators, employees, victims, most individuals) usually only need to prove **negligence**. Much higher risk — minimize naming and characterizing private individuals unless essential and already public in the record.
- **Companies can be defamed** too, and statements about products can be trade libel.
- **Defamation by implication:** true facts arranged to imply a false conclusion (juxtaposition, loaded questions, "coincidentally…", suggestive visuals, music-less but ominous framing). Reviewers must read the *implication*, not only the sentences.

## 2. Opinion vs fact
- Pure opinion is protected, but "opinion" that implies undisclosed false facts is not ("In my opinion he stole the money" = a factual claim).
- Safe opinion: clearly evaluative, based on disclosed true facts ("Given the $3 million penalty and the guilty pleas, it's hard to call this a rogue-employee story.").
- Rhetorical hyperbole ("a disaster") is usually safe; specific accusations of crimes are not.

## 3. Fair report privilege — our most important protection
- Accurately reporting what an **official proceeding or public record** says (complaints, indictments, court orders, agency releases) is privileged in most US jurisdictions **if the report is fair, accurate and attributed**.
- The privilege is lost if we drop the attribution ("the complaint alleges" → "he did"), cherry-pick to change meaning, or add our own unsupported embellishment.
- A complaint is a record of what a party *claims*. Always attribute and, where available, include the response or denial.
- Press releases by prosecutors are attributed as such ("the Justice Department said").

## 4. Criminal vs civil vs regulatory vocabulary
- **Charged / indicted:** accusation. **Pleaded guilty / convicted:** established guilt for those counts only. **Acquitted / dismissed:** not proven — never "innocent" unless a court said so, never "guilty anyway".
- **Settled:** no admission unless the settlement says so. "Without admitting or denying" (SEC) means exactly that.
- **Deferred prosecution agreement:** not a conviction; the company typically admits a statement of facts — quote what it admitted, no more.
- **Liable (civil):** only after judgment or verdict. "Found liable" ≠ "committed a crime".
- Sentences: say what the court imposed; appeals pending must be mentioned when material.

## 5. People in the story
- **Name** people who are parties, officials, executives acting in role, convicted defendants, or public figures. **Describe by role** victims, junior employees, uncharged individuals, family members.
- When someone appears in a story about wrongdoing but was **not charged / not a party / not found liable**, say so clearly at that point.
- Photos of real people: editorial use of a real, sourced photo is normal; never generate or alter a real person's face or expression to imply an event.

## 6. Copyright & fair use (for visuals and documents)
- **Fair use factors:** purpose (commentary/criticism/news, transformative), nature of the work, amount used, market effect. Documentary commentary with short, relevant excerpts is the strongest case.
- **US federal government works** (DOJ releases, court opinions written by judges) are not copyrighted. **Filings written by private lawyers** can be — show excerpts for commentary, don't reproduce whole documents.
- **Logos:** editorial use to identify a company is fine; never imply endorsement or sponsorship.
- **News photos / TV clips:** licensed or fair-use-justified only; Content ID claims can demonetize. Prefer public records, Creative Commons (with attribution), own graphics, own generations.
- **Creative Commons:** BY and BY-SA require attribution (author, license, link) — on screen or in the description.

## 7. YouTube policies that bite this format
- **Inauthentic / mass-produced content (monetization):** content that is repetitive, template-driven or mass-produced with little original value can lose monetization. Defense: real research, original script and analysis, varied structure, real documents, clear editorial voice.
- **Altered or synthetic content disclosure:** realistic AI-generated visuals that a viewer could mistake for real people/events must be disclosed in YouTube Studio. Our rule: never make realistic fakes of real people/events in the first place; still tick the disclosure when realistic AI imagery is used.
- **Harassment & cyberbullying:** no content that targets an identifiable private individual, reveals addresses, or encourages audiences to contact people.
- **Violent / graphic content:** describe crimes factually, no gratuitous detail, no graphic imagery.
- **Misleading metadata:** title/thumbnail must reflect the content; sensational claims the video doesn't support can lead to removal and hurt retention anyway.

## 8. Red-flag wording (reviewers: search for these)
"scam", "fraud", "stole", "criminal", "illegal", "lied", "cover-up", "knew", "admitted", "guilty", "corrupt", "illegally", "secretly", "exposed" — each needs a status that supports it (conviction, admission, finding) or explicit attribution ("prosecutors say", "the complaint calls it").
Implication patterns: "Coincidence? …", "Nobody asked why…", "What were they hiding?", ominous juxtaposition of a person's face with a crime they were not charged with.

## 9. Safe patterns
- "According to the indictment, …" / "The complaint claims … DistroKid denies the allegations."
- "He pleaded guilty to …" (state the count) / "A jury found …" / "The judge ruled that …, a decision X is appealing."
- "The settlement did not include an admission of wrongdoing."
- "We contacted X for comment" — only if actually done; otherwise cite their public statement.
