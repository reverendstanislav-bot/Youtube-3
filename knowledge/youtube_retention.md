# YouTube retention — how long-form documentaries keep viewers

Working knowledge for writer, critic, hook-doctor, visual-director, retention-editor. Not dogma — every rule serves one goal: **the viewer never has a reason to leave.**

## 1. What YouTube actually rewards
- Recommendation is driven by **viewer satisfaction**: click-through (CTR) × how long people watch (average view duration, AVD / average percentage viewed, APV) × whether they come back, like, and keep watching YouTube afterwards.
- A high-CTR title that the video does not pay off → early abandonment → the video dies. **Packaging promise and first 30 s must match.**
- For 15–22 min business documentaries, healthy targets: **>70% still watching at 0:30**, **APV 40–55%**, CTR 4–10%. The first minute decides most of the curve.
- The retention graph has three typical failure shapes:
  1. **Cliff in the first 30 s** — the hook did not confirm the click, or the intro wastes time.
  2. **Steady slide** — no escalation; every minute feels like the previous one.
  3. **Dips at transitions** — "section headers", recaps, backstory dumps, sponsor-like tangents.
- Bumps (re-watch spikes) appear at reveals, documents on screen, numbers, and moments viewers want to check. Design them on purpose.

## 2. The first 60 seconds
Structure that works for this format:
1. **Cold open (0–10 s):** the most arresting true moment, number, document or contradiction. No logo, no greeting, no "in this video".
2. **Promise (10–25 s):** confirm the title/thumbnail in plain words and raise the question the video will answer.
3. **Stakes (25–45 s):** why it matters — money, people hurt, who could lose what.
4. **Open loop (by 60 s):** a question or a teased reveal that is paid off later ("…and the document that ended it was signed by someone nobody expected").
Never: history lessons, definitions, "before we begin", channel intro, asking to subscribe, listing the chapters.

## 3. Keeping them in the middle
- **Re-hook every 60–90 s:** a new question, a reversal, a new number, a new character, a document reveal. Each section should end with a reason to watch the next one ("But that wasn't the problem. The problem was in paragraph 48.").
- **But / therefore, never "and then":** each beat should turn the previous one (South Park rule). A list of events is a Wikipedia article, not a film.
- **Open loops stack:** open a new loop before closing the old one; close every loop by the end.
- **Payoff frequency:** a small payoff (fact, number, reveal) at least every 2 minutes; a big one at ~⅓, ~⅔ and the end.
- **Density without fatigue:** one idea per sentence, one claim per beat. After a dense legal/financial explanation, give a concrete human or visual beat.
- **Specific beats general:** "4,562 tracks in twelve months" beats "thousands of songs". A named person beats "an employee".
- **Remove anything the viewer would skip:** recaps longer than one sentence, repeated points, throat-clearing transitions ("Now let's talk about…").
- **Mid-video sag (40–60%)** is where most documentaries lose people — place the strongest reversal there, not at the end only.

## 4. Visual pacing (for picture planning and edit review)
- Change what the viewer is processing roughly every **3–6 s** in normal passages, faster (2–3 s) in tense sequences, slower (6–8 s) on a document the viewer must read.
- A shot held >8 s without motion or new information is a dead zone. Fix with push-in, highlight, second layer, cut.
- Vary the *function* of shots (place → evidence → explanation → person → consequence), not just the layout. Three similar frames in a row feel like a slideshow.
- Put text on screen only when it adds information the narration does not (a number, a date, a quote, a status). Never duplicate full sentences of narration as titles.
- Numbers and quotes on screen are retention bumps — give them 2–4 s to land.

## 5. Narration rhythm (TTS)
- ~150–165 words per minute. Short sentences carry tension; vary length for rhythm (short-short-long).
- Put the new information at the end of the sentence ("The fine was three million dollars." not "Three million dollars was the fine that…").
- Avoid parentheticals, nested clauses and lists of more than three — TTS flattens them.
- Pauses are free drama: a paragraph break before a reveal.

## 6. Ending
- Deliver the promised payoff clearly before the final minute.
- End on consequence + resonance (what it says about business, power, money), then the channel line. If the matter is unresolved, say what would decide it.
- No long outro; the end screen appears in the last 20 s — the final line should make the next video feel like a continuation.

## 7. Shorts (cut from the long-form)
- The first second must be self-explanatory: start on the strongest line, not on context.
- 25–60 s, one idea, one payoff, a closing line that makes the viewer want the full story.
- Captions large and centered in the safe band; motion in the first frame.
- A Short that loops cleanly (last line leads into the first) gets extra views.

## 8. Checklist for reviewers
- Does 0:00–0:10 contain something a viewer has not seen in competing videos?
- Is the title promise confirmed by 0:25?
- Where would I get bored? (Mark timestamps.)
- Is there a reversal in the middle third?
- Is every section's last line a reason to continue?
- Could any 30-second block be removed without loss? Then remove it.
