### 1. Fortnite: billing design
**Three titles:** “The Fortnite Buttons Behind a$245 Million Refund Fund”; “Why Fortnite Had to Fund$245 Million in Refunds”; “Fortnite's Expensive Purchase Problem.”
**Promise:** show how an interface decision became a consumer-money case and a concrete remedy.
**Thumbnail:** Fortnite-recognizable licensed/cleared game image, one purchase-control detail, large“$245 M”; avoid unrelated children/handcuffs or a fictional evidence screenshot.
**Hook:** “Fortnite was free to play. But unwanted purchases led to a$245 million refund settlement. The question wasn't just what players bought. It was what happened when they pressed a button.”
**Conflict/caveats:** user consent versus interface/billing practices; attribute FTC allegations and distinguish the separate privacy penalty. No assertion every player was overcharged.
**Why potentially stronger:** immediate viewer relevance and a visible mechanism reduce the context burden of DistroKid. Hypothesis, not demonstrated demand.
**Effort:** estimated 28–44 person-hours total, including 7–11 research/legal,3–5 script,12–18 visual/edit,6–10 QC/packaging; rights-dependent gameplay adds uncertainty.

### 2. Amazon Prime: cancellation
**Three titles:** “Amazon's$2.5 Billion Prime Cancellation Settlement”; “The Cost of Making Prime Hard to Cancel”; “Why Amazon Agreed to Pay$2.5 Billion Over Prime.”
**Promise:** follow a familiar signup/cancellation journey into the legal and financial consequences.
**Thumbnail:** Prime wordmark, one plainly illustrative cancellation path, large“$2.5 B”; no counterfeit internal email or claim that a tiny button alone caused the entire outcome.
**Hook:** “Signing up for Prime was easy. The FTC said leaving was another story. Amazon eventually agreed to a$2.5 billion settlement. How did a subscription become a case this expensive?”
**Conflict/caveats:** consent and cancellation friction; settlement terms versus disputed conduct; redress fund is not cash already received by every customer.
**Why potentially stronger:** a recognizable everyday decision gives viewers personal stakes before legal context.
**Effort:** estimated 30–46 hours:8–12 research/legal,4–6 script,12–18 visual/edit,6–10 QC/packaging. Historical UI must be dated or explicitly illustrative.

### 3. Waymo/Uber (locked 005)
**Three titles:** “The Engineer at the Center of Google's Fight With Uber”; “The Files Behind Waymo's Battle With Uber”; “Google's Engineer Left. Then Came the Trade-Secret Fight.”
**Promise:** trace exactly which conduct was admitted, what rivals alleged, and what consequences actually followed.
**Thumbnail:** authentic engineer portrait with rights check, Google/Waymo and Uber identifiers, one file-transfer motif clearly editorial;“THE FILES” rather than an unsupported cash sum.
**Hook:** “An engineer left Google's self-driving project. Confidential files became the center of a fight with Uber. He later pleaded guilty to trade-secret theft. But his criminal case and Uber's settlement were different stories.”
**Conflict/caveats:** talent acquisition versus confidential technology; distinguish admitted file/count from alleged 14,000 files; do not call Uber criminally convicted; include pardon.
**Why potentially stronger:** a protagonist and a departure decision create causal tension more easily than procedural exposition. Existing competition limits novelty.
**Effort:** estimated 40–64 hours:12–18 research/legal,5–8 script,16–26 visual/edit,7–12 QC/packaging; archival rights and multiple proceedings dominate.

**Promise:** make the two-contract reversal understandable without pretending the underlying antitrust dispute is decided.
**Thumbnail:** Steam identity plus old“ARBITRATION” and new“COURT” routes, one reversal arrow; no fake$20 M invoice or“YOU LOST YOUR GAMES” assertion.

### 5. Apple ebooks
**Three titles:** “The Ebook Deal That Cost Apple$400 Million in Customer Payments”; “How Apple Lost the Ebook Price Fight”; “The Contract Behind Apple's Ebook Antitrust Case.”
**Promise:** explain how a pricing contract changed who set prices and why customers later received money.
**Thumbnail:** Apple device/book price labels connected by one contract page;“WHO SETS THE PRICE?”; no fake judge quote or$400 M“fine.”
**Hook:** “Apple entered ebooks with a new pricing deal. A court found an illegal conspiracy. Years later, the end of Apple's appeal route triggered$400 million for customers. What changed inside the contract?”
**Conflict/caveats:** pricing authority and publisher/platform incentives; distinguish trial findings, appeals and settlement damages; Supreme Court refusal to review is not a merits verdict.
**Why potentially stronger:** everyday prices plus a concealed contract support the brand's repeatable promise; age reduces urgency, so distinctive evidence is necessary.
**Effort:** estimated 28–42 hours:7–11 research/legal,4–6 script,11–16 visual/edit,6–9 QC/packaging.

## Practical selection gate
Before approving any new story, require one-page evidence-first pitch: one-sentence conflict, specific consequence, three primary events, opposing position, one unique mechanism, authentic visual path, provisional hook/title/thumbnail alignment and all-in labor/credit ceiling. Have an independent reviewer explain it in one sentence without legal vocabulary. If they cannot, revise the pitch before paid media.

Effort ranges are planning estimates assuming the existing tools/voice/style and one major revision, not measured historical labor. Credits must be separately quoted against exact approved inputs. A source-heavy or rights-blocked story can exceed these ranges; no paid job is proposed by this report.
