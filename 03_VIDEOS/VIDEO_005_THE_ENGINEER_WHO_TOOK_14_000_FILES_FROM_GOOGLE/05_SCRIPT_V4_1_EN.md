## A00 — THE SECOND PAYMENT

**A00-P01**

In 2018, Uber ended its courtroom fight with Waymo in a deal involving shares valued at about $245 million. Case closed. Except four years later, Uber paid Google again—and this time, the amount was sealed. Why was money changing hands after that famous settlement? The answer starts with roughly 14,000 files one engineer copied from Google's internal systems.

**A00-P02**

It's tempting to hear those numbers and imagine two punishments for the same proven theft. That's not what happened. The first settlement ended a corporate lawsuit without a jury verdict. The second payment followed a different legal fight—one that grew out of a contract signed before Uber bought a small startup. But the money itself was paid under a new settlement in 2022.

**A00-P03**

To follow the story, imagine two case files. One holds Waymo's lawsuit against Uber. The other holds an engineer's duties to his former employer, Google—and his disputed demand that Uber cover certain liabilities. Some of the names overlap. The money doesn't. Mix those cases together, and you'll get the biggest question wrong: who paid whom, and why?

**A00-P04**

The engineer was Anthony Levandowski. In March 2020, a California court would confirm a personal judgment against him exceeding $179 million. Hours later, he would file for bankruptcy protection. But that's still years away. For now, he's at Google, working near technology every company in the self-driving race wants to understand. And he has access most competitors don't.

**A00-P05**

Forget the billion-dollar headlines for a moment. Forget the Uber lawsuit. The first decisive fact in this story is much smaller: a set of files copied from an internal Google project. What happened to those files would raise an extraordinary accusation. What it could actually prove is a different question entirely.

## A01 — THE 14,000 FILES

**A01-P01**

It's late 2015. Google's self-driving project is trying to solve a brutal engineering problem: how does a car understand the world around it? One important tool is LiDAR, which uses laser measurements to map nearby objects. But the value isn't contained in a single futuristic sensor. It's also in the team's accumulated designs, testing, and confidential engineering records.

**A01-P02**

Levandowski knows this field from the inside. To a rival, that kind of experience is enormously attractive. And there's nothing automatically wrong with hiring an experienced engineer. But experience and an old employer's confidential files are not the same thing. You can take your skills to a new job. That doesn't give the new company permission to take the old company's protected information.

**A01-P03**

In December 2015, Google engineer Anthony Levandowski downloaded roughly 14,000 internal self-driving project files and moved the collection to a personal computer, according to the Justice Department. That establishes the copying. It does not, by itself, establish that Uber later received all those files—or used them in its own technology.

**A01-P04**

There's our first answer. The download happened. But downloading information, transferring it to a rival, and building that information into a rival's product are three different claims. Each requires its own evidence. That's a less convenient story than a headline declaring one company stole everything. It's also the only way to understand what happens next.

**A01-P05**

In January 2016, Levandowski leaves Google. A young company called Otto is taking shape, focused on autonomous trucks. The business pitch practically writes itself: take expertise developed in self-driving vehicles and apply it to the massive trucks hauling America's freight. To a buyer in a technology race, Otto offers more than an idea. It offers people who might help close a gap quickly.

## A02 — THE $680 MILLION HEADLINE

**A02-P01**

Enter Uber. It isn't merely calling an engineer with a job offer. In February 2016, Uber and Otto have preliminary acquisition terms. Suddenly, the central question isn't just what this team might build. It's what a buyer might inherit along with that promise. Talent can move from company to company. Unresolved legal exposure can follow.

**A02-P02**

The purchase makes a spectacular headline. Otto's potential deal value is reported at around $680 million. That sounds like Uber showed up with nearly three-quarters of a billion dollars in cash. But the Delaware court record includes a detail that changes the entire picture. What was actually paid up front was nowhere near that headline number.

**A02-P03**

Uber's 2016 Otto acquisition was described as potentially worth up to $680 million. But Delaware court records put the initial cash payment at just $100,000. Much of the potential consideration involved equity tied to future conditions. So the claim that Uber handed this engineer $680 million in cash is simply not what the records say.

**A02-P04**

That's the difference between an acquisition's possible future value and cash paid on day one. Uber was betting on what a young team might accomplish. Some of the upside still had to be earned. But the team's past was already real. So while the potential reward depended on the future, the legal risks didn't wait for anyone to hit a milestone.

**A02-P05**

By April 2016, Uber had agreed to acquire Otto. Alongside that deal came a separate indemnification agreement covering certain employees who had left Google. Put simply, it addressed when one party might have to cover certain legal costs or liabilities faced by another. Not every bill. Not automatically. But years later, the exact limits of this agreement would matter far more than the flashy acquisition figure.

**A02-P06**

Here comes the uncomfortable part. Uber didn't proceed without asking questions. Before the acquisition closed, it hired forensic investigators to examine concerns about information retained by former Google employees. That's not the same as proving the deal was illegal. But it means the potential problem was being examined before the purchase was complete. What did those investigators find? And why did the deal go ahead?

## A03 — THE INVESTIGATION BEFORE THE DEAL

**A03-P01**

The firm was Stroz Friedberg, specialists in digital forensics. Their job was to look at devices and evidence of retained information. Uber brought them in after the preliminary deal terms but before closing the acquisition. That timing matters. This wasn't a surprise discovered after the fact. The buyer was trying to understand what risks might already be attached to the team it wanted.

**A03-P02**

Later, in a Delaware shareholder lawsuit, a plaintiff alleged that there were serious internal concerns, including questions about what company lawyers had found and what directors had been told. But those are allegations in a corporate governance case. They are not a criminal verdict against Uber's board, and they don't establish that each director knew about a particular theft.

**A03-P03**

Now picture the business decision. Walk away from a team that might accelerate your technology program? Or close the deal while troubling questions about the team's former employer remain? Court records describe diligence, concern, and negotiations over how certain risks would be allocated. They do not give us permission to invent private conversations or declare that everyone in the room expected a crime.

**A03-P04**

Before buying Otto, Uber hired Stroz Friedberg to investigate concerns about former Google employees. The review raised serious questions. But Stroz's final report found no evidence that Google's confidential information retained by Anthony Levandowski had been transferred to Otto or third parties. That's a narrow finding—not proof that every possible violation was ruled out.

**A03-P05**

So neither easy version works. You can't say the investigators found nothing worrying, therefore there was no risk. But you can't say retained documents automatically mean Uber received Google's technology either. What the inquiry raised, and what it failed to establish, are different things. And that uncertainty was now part of a decision with potentially enormous consequences.

**A03-P06**

Uber's board approved the acquisition, and the deal closed in August 2016. Levandowski took a senior role in Uber's autonomous-driving program. The questions hadn't disappeared. They'd moved inside a much bigger story. A few months later, something happened that didn't require a whistleblower or a hack. Somebody simply sent an email to the wrong company.

## A04 — THE EMAIL SENT TO THE WRONG COMPANY

**A04-P01**

December 2016. A supplier sends an email intended for Uber. It lands at Google instead. Attached are circuit-board drawings related to self-driving technology. According to later court accounts, the drawings allegedly resembled Google's designs. That resemblance was not a finding of theft. But an unexpected look at a rival's engineering was enough to raise a new, very specific question.

**A04-P02**

The email didn't contain Levandowski's entire collection of 14,000 files. And resemblance alone wasn't proof that Uber had used protected Google information. But think about the change in perspective. First, Google had a former engineer who copied confidential materials. Now it had engineering drawings intended for Uber that appeared, according to the allegations, uncomfortably familiar.

**A04-P03**

In December 2016, a supplier accidentally sent Google an email meant for Uber. It contained circuit-board drawings that were later alleged to resemble Google's designs. That gave Google a new reason to investigate. But the email itself did not prove Uber had used Waymo trade secrets that Waymo accused it of misappropriating.

**A04-P04**

In February 2017, Waymo—the company built around Google's self-driving project—sued Uber and related Otto entities in federal court. Waymo alleged that trade secrets, including information involving LiDAR, had been misappropriated. Uber denied incorporating Waymo's trade secrets into its self-driving technology. Now the question wasn't just what people suspected. It was what the evidence could support in court.

**A04-P05**

And the court's early response wasn't a simple win for either side. A judge found grounds for certain temporary restrictions, but that did not mean every Waymo accusation had been proved. Meanwhile, another question was developing. What if potentially important information had been stored on media nobody could now inspect?

## A05 — FIVE DISKS AND THE VERDICT THAT NEVER CAME

**A05-P01**

In May 2017, federal judge William Alsup issued a preliminary order. He treated evidence that Levandowski had downloaded substantial Google material as serious and imposed specified restrictions, including on his LiDAR work. But preliminary relief is not a final verdict. And the court did not accept every theory or every asserted trade secret in the same way.

**A05-P02**

At this point, you might expect a clean chain of proof. If documents were copied, show where they went. If a rival used them, show the result. Real litigation is messier. A file on a personal device and a trade secret used inside a competitor's vehicle are separated by evidentiary steps. And one missing step can change everything a jury may conclude.

**A05-P03**

Then an older conversation became important. Back in March 2016, Levandowski had told Uber executives he possessed five disks containing Google information. Uber said he later reported destroying them, and that Uber never received the disks. Their contents could not be independently verified. The fight now included a different issue: how late Uber had disclosed those facts to Waymo.

**A05-P04**

Five disks containing Google information. According to Uber, Anthony Levandowski told its executives about them in March 2016, then later said he'd destroyed them. In January 2018, a judge said jurors could draw an unfavorable inference from Uber's late disclosure—but they didn't have to. The disks' contents remained unverified.

**A05-P05**

That distinction changes the story. The judge did not declare that those disks held all 14,000 files, or a smoking-gun transfer into Uber's systems. But he also didn't wave away Uber's delayed disclosure. Jurors would have been allowed to weigh it against the company. Allowed, not required. Two words that make a major difference when the case is still unresolved.

**A05-P06**

In February 2018, the case reached trial. At last, it looked as if a jury might decide what had been used, what could be proved, and what liability would follow. Except the ending the courtroom was moving toward never arrived. Before there was a jury verdict, the corporate opponents made a deal.

## A06 — THE STOCK SETTLEMENT THAT WASN'T THE END

**A06-P01**

On February 9, 2018, Waymo and Uber settled. The lawsuit was dismissed with prejudice, ending those claims in that case. But a settlement isn't a jury ruling that every allegation in the complaint was true. There was no jury verdict on liability before the parties agreed to end the fight. And other disputes were still alive.
