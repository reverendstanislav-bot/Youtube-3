## 0. Story promise and thesis
**Click promise:** What happened to roughly 14,000 copied Google self-driving files, why did an Uber acquisition create an expensive corporate showdown, what was actually proven, and how did Uber and Waymo later become commercial partners?

**Audience-driving question:** "Was Uber buying a breakthrough—or buying a very expensive legal and business risk?"

**Proposed thesis (editorial inference, not court judgment):** the acquisition of specialized talent and technology can also acquire disputes over who owns information and who is allowed to use it. The criminal story of one engineer, the settled civil corporate case and Uber's later business model must stay separate. The true business-ending is counterintuitive: Uber eventually exited ownership of its self-driving unit and offered rides operated by former adversary Waymo through its app. The record does **not** establish that the civil settlement caused either later decision. [C009–C023, C024–C038]

**True financial question:** what did the **deal potentially promise**, what did the **civil settlement transfer in stock**, and what was a **separate later corporate investment**? Never sum all numbers into a fake "total cost."

## 6. Hidden mechanism explained with minimal law
"Trade secret" in story = confidential business information claimed to create competitive value, not just any clever employee's knowledge. **Owning talent ≠ owning every document that talent previously accessed.** Litigation tests specific information, acquisition diligence and alleged use, not possession of "robotaxi ideas" in general. 2017 relief was **preliminary** (court deciding what to prevent while case pending), 2018 disposition was a **settlement** (parties ended case), and 2020 conviction was an individual's **plea** to a defined count. These are enough concepts; avoid a separate legal class on trade-secret doctrine.
