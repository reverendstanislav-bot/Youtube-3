## A08 — WHY UBER PAID GOOGLE AGAIN

**A08-P01**

The answer wasn't buried in a secret verdict against Uber. Levandowski's claim under the 2016 indemnification agreement became part of his bankruptcy litigation. Uber contested both its reach and what it might require. Neither side had a court order declaring that Uber owed his entire personal judgment under that old contract.

**A08-P02**

Instead, in 2022, Google, Uber, and Levandowski reached a new global settlement. This ended the dispute through negotiated terms rather than a ruling that the contested 2016 indemnity was enforceable. One part of the new arrangement required money to move directly from Uber to Google.

**A08-P03**

Court records confirm that direct payment was made by May 20, 2022. Here's the strange detail: the principal amount was kept under seal. An earlier settlement's stock value was public; the later payment's size wasn't. But the absence of a disclosed number doesn't mean the transfer was imaginary.

**A08-P04**

In 2022, Uber paid Google again. The amount is sealed. This wasn't a secret Waymo jury verdict: court records connect the payment to a separate settlement involving Anthony Levandowski. Another $2 million was provided to fund his bankruptcy plan. Those were distinct payments—not a disclosed price for a second finding of stolen technology.

**A08-P05**

And that's the answer promised at the start. A dispute over the 2016 indemnification agreement led to a 2022 settlement, but the actual Uber-to-Google payment was made under that new agreement. A bankruptcy court later said explicitly it was not performance of the old indemnification contract. The court did not decide Uber had to pay Levandowski's $179 million judgment under that clause.

**A08-P06**

One final number is still missing: the sealed amount of Uber's direct payment. It cannot be replaced with the personal $179 million judgment or the separate $2 million for the bankruptcy plan. But the 2022 agreement wasn't the last time the payment appeared in court.

## A09 — THE COST THAT OUTLIVED THE DEAL

**A09-P01**

In 2025, a bankruptcy court confronted a new question: how should Uber's main payment be treated for federal tax purposes? It concluded the payment counted as gross income to Levandowski. That wasn't a calculation of his final tax bill, and it didn't undermine the validity of the settlement.

**A09-P02**

Think about that timeline. Levandowski copied Google's files in 2015. Uber bought Otto in 2016. The high-profile Waymo case ended in 2018. Yet a court was still parsing the consequences of a separate Uber payment in 2025. The story had outlived its apparent ending by seven years.

**A09-P03**

The numbers cannot honestly be turned into one giant loss. The up-to-$680-million Otto transaction involved conditional value. The roughly $245-million Waymo deal involved stock. The $179-million judgment was personal. The separate Uber-to-Google payment was real—but its amount is not public.

**A09-P04**

What ties those outcomes together isn't a single verdict finding Uber responsible for all 14,000 downloaded files. It's a sequence of choices: an engineer copied documents, a buyer evaluated risks, a company acquired the team, and years later the parties resolved a different contractual fight.

**A09-P05**

So when you hear a giant acquisition price, there's a second question worth asking: what liabilities travel with the people being bought? Uber's 2018 settlement ended one lawsuit. Its 2022 settlement resolved another dispute and sent money to Google. The second amount remains sealed. The cost of a deal doesn't always end when its headline does.
