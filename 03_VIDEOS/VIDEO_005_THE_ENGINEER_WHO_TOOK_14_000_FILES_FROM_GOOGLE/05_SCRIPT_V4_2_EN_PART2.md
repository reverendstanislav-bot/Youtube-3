## A03 — THE INVESTIGATION BEFORE THE PURCHASE

**A03-P01**

The firm Uber hired was Stroz Friedberg. Its specialists examined digital devices and the possibility that former Google employees had retained sensitive materials. The timing is the important part: the review came after the preliminary acquisition terms but before Otto officially became part of Uber.

**A03-P02**

The inquiry raised concerns. Later, a shareholder lawsuit alleged that company lawyers had expressed reservations and that directors might not have received every warning. Those were the plaintiff's allegations about the acquisition process, not a court finding that the entire Uber board knowingly participated in stealing trade secrets.

**A03-P03**

Now the business decision had real stakes. A promising engineering team could help Uber compete faster. But a purchase might also import claims over confidential files. Due diligence couldn't give the board a guarantee about every future lawsuit. It could identify evidence and the limits of what investigators had actually established.

**A03-P04**

Uber investigated Google-data concerns before buying Otto. Its forensic firm, Stroz Friedberg, reported serious issues—but found no evidence that confidential Google information retained by Anthony Levandowski had been transferred to Otto or third parties. That didn't clear every possible violation. Uber nevertheless completed the acquisition. The warning existed; proof of that specific transfer did not.

**A03-P05**

Uber's board approved the deal. Otto became part of Uber in August 2016, and Levandowski entered its self-driving program. An important question remained unresolved: if the files had been copied, could anyone show that Uber's technology actually contained Google's protected work?

**A03-P06**

Then someone outside both companies made an extraordinary mistake. A supplier sent technical drawings meant for Uber to Google instead. The next phase of the case began with an email that arrived in the wrong inbox.

## A04 — ONE EMAIL SENT TO THE WRONG COMPANY

**A04-P01**

December 2016. Google receives a supplier email that was supposed to go to Uber. Attached are circuit-board drawings connected to autonomous vehicles. According to later court accounts, Google saw similarities to its own designs. Suddenly, suspicions about what an engineer had copied were accompanied by a glimpse of engineering work intended for the buyer.

**A04-P02**

Was it the missing link? Not by itself. The drawings were not a copy of Levandowski's entire 14,000-file collection, and the resemblance was alleged. Still, the mistaken delivery gave Google a concrete reason to examine what its former engineer and its rival might be doing.

**A04-P03**

One wrong email helped move a tech rivalry toward court. In December 2016, a supplier sent Google circuit-board drawings intended for Uber. Google suspected similarities to its designs. The email didn't prove Uber used stolen secrets—but it helped prompt further investigation. By February 2017, Waymo had filed a federal lawsuit against Uber and Otto.

**A04-P04**

Waymo, which grew out of Google's self-driving project, alleged theft and misuse of confidential technology, including LiDAR-related trade secrets. Uber denied incorporating Waymo's trade secrets into its own systems. These were opposing positions, and the courtroom had to decide what the evidence could support.

**A04-P05**

In May 2017, Judge William Alsup imposed limited preliminary restrictions, including measures affecting Levandowski's LiDAR work. That was not a final verdict proving every allegation. More importantly for the next turn, attention shifted to information that had once existed on physical storage devices—but could no longer be examined.

## A05 — FIVE DISKS AND THE MISSING VERDICT

**A05-P01**

Months before the acquisition closed, in March 2016, Levandowski had told Uber executives about five disks holding Google information. According to Uber, he later said he'd destroyed them; the company said it never received the disks. Their contents could not be verified. Now that earlier conversation mattered in the lawsuit.

**A05-P02**

The problem wasn't just that the disks were unavailable. Uber's disclosure of what it knew came late. In January 2018, the judge considered what a jury could reasonably infer from that delay. The legal answer was narrower—and more consequential—than saying anyone had proved the disks contained a stolen product.

**A05-P03**

The court permitted jurors to take Uber's delayed disclosure into account against the company. It didn't order them to assume every Waymo accusation was true. And it didn't establish exactly what had been on the missing disks. That left an evidentiary question hanging over an approaching trial.

**A05-P04**

Five disks containing Google information became a major trial issue. Uber said Anthony Levandowski had described them in March 2016 and later reported destroying them. In January 2018, a judge ruled jurors could draw an unfavorable inference from Uber's late disclosure—but they didn't have to. Nobody established exactly what the disks contained.

**A05-P05**

Imagine the choice facing jurors: how much weight should they give information disclosed late, when the disks themselves could not be inspected? That was a real question the trial might have explored. It was not an automatic finding that Uber's self-driving system used every downloaded file.

**A05-P06**

In February 2018, the Waymo–Uber trial began. It looked as though a jury might finally answer what had been used and what could be proved. Instead, before the jury reached a verdict, the companies made a deal. Its headline value was massive. Its legal meaning was much narrower.
