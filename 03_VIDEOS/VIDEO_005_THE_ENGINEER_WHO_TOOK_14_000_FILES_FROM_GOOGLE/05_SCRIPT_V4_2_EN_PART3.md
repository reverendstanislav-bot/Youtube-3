## A06 — THE $245 MILLION ENDING THAT WASN'T

**A06-P01**

February 9, 2018. Waymo and Uber agreed to settle, and the corporate case was dismissed with prejudice. The announced value associated with the settlement was roughly $245 million in Uber shares. Headlines could finally announce an ending. But the jury had never rendered a verdict on whether Uber was liable for the allegations Waymo brought.

**A06-P02**

$245 million sounds like a jury penalty. It wasn't. In February 2018, Uber settled Waymo's trade-secret lawsuit using shares valued at about $245 million at the time. The jury issued no liability verdict, and Uber denied incorporating Waymo's secrets. This was a negotiated settlement—not a criminal fine or court-awarded damages.

**A06-P03**

Uber's leadership expressed regret about how it handled the Otto acquisition. That did not turn the allegations into judicial findings. Waymo's case ended, but the settlement didn't cancel the former engineer's separate obligations to Google, and it didn't resolve the indemnification agreement Uber had signed while buying Otto.

**A06-P04**

Here is where the story splits. One lawsuit asked whether Waymo's secrets had been improperly used by Uber. Another dispute concerned Levandowski's obligations to Google as a former employee, including issues related to recruiting colleagues. Same central figure, different legal claims. Only one of those stories ended in February 2018.

**A06-P05**

Two years later, the personal proceeding reached a result no acquisition press release had anticipated. The debt fell on Levandowski himself. And it was so large that he filed for bankruptcy protection within hours of the court's decision.

## A07 — A PERSONAL JUDGMENT THAT CAME BACK TO UBER

**A07-P01**

Google had pursued its claims against Levandowski through arbitration. They were rooted in employment and contractual duties, not a jury finding that Uber used every downloaded file. When a California court confirmed the award, the consequences became personal rather than just another corporate headline.

**A07-P02**

On March 4, 2020, the court confirmed a civil judgment against Levandowski of $179,047,998.64. He filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy hours later. The judgment wasn't against Uber. But the bankruptcy would force a new confrontation over a contract signed while Uber was acquiring Otto.

**A07-P03**

There's an important second proceeding here. Federal prosecutors had charged Levandowski with 33 criminal counts over alleged theft or attempted theft of trade secrets. The eventual outcome was not 33 convictions. He pleaded guilty to one count concerning a specific confidential Google document—not a conviction for every file in the 14,000-file collection.

**A07-P04**

In August 2020, Levandowski was sentenced to 18 months in prison in that criminal case. In January 2021, he received a presidential pardon. That changed the federal criminal story; it did not erase the separate civil judgment already confirmed against him. The personal financial problem remained.

**A07-P05**

Why would any of this lead back to Uber? Because of the conditional indemnification agreement negotiated before the Otto acquisition. Levandowski argued that Uber should cover the liability connected to Google. Uber disputed whether the agreement required that coverage. The court hadn't simply ordered Uber to pay the $179 million.

**A07-P06**

So one question survived the corporation's 2018 settlement: who would bear the cost of the engineer's separate dispute with Google? And an even sharper question followed. Would the contract itself decide it—or would the parties have to negotiate another ending?
