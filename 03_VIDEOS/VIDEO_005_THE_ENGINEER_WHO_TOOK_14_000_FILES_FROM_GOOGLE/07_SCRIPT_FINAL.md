## A00 — THE ENGINEER, THE JUDGMENT, THE SECOND PAYMENT

**A00-P01**

Anthony Levandowski copied roughly 14,000 Google self-driving files. A separate dispute over his duties to Google later produced a personal civil judgment exceeding $179 million. He filed for bankruptcy hours later. The debt wasn't Uber's. Yet Uber would make a sealed payment to Google in 2022 under a different settlement.

**A00-P02**

Uber had already settled Waymo's trade-secret lawsuit in 2018, using shares valued at about $245 million. There was no jury verdict. So why was money moving from Uber to Google four years later? And how did one engineer's departure become a problem that lasted well beyond the trial everyone remembered?

**A00-P03**

Before Otto became part of Uber, the deal had already drawn a forensic investigation and a contract about certain legal risks. Later, a supplier's mistake would give Google a fresh reason to investigate. What would matter was not one spectacular number, but what each decision left unresolved.

**A00-P04**

The tempting version is simple: stolen files, an expensive acquisition, a guilty company. The documents tell a more complicated story. They show copying. They show warnings before a deal. But they do not establish that Uber used all 14,000 files. That difference will matter once a jury gets close to deciding the case.

**A00-P05**

Start in December 2015, inside Google's self-driving operation. Levandowski was an experienced engineer in a race that could transform transportation. He also had access to internal files a competitor could not simply purchase. And he was about to leave.

## A01 — THE DOWNLOAD AND THE DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS

**A01-P01**

Google's autonomous-driving team was developing technology to help vehicles sense the world. One tool was LiDAR: laser-based scanning that helps a vehicle detect nearby objects. The real competitive edge involved years of engineering decisions, drawings, and testing. That's why the line between an engineer's personal experience and confidential company material mattered so much.

**A01-P02**

On December 11, 2015, Levandowski downloaded a large collection from Google's internal project repository. Days later, the files were transferred to a personal computer. The Justice Department would later describe the collection as approximately 14,000 files. The number was real. The question was what happened after the copy.

**A01-P03**

He copied about 14,000 Google project files. But here's the key distinction: the Justice Department described a download to Anthony Levandowski's personal computer, not proof that Uber used 14,000 files in its cars. Copying, passing documents to a new company, and building a product with them are separate claims requiring separate evidence.

**A01-P04**

In mid-January 2016, while he still worked at Google, Levandowski founded what became Otto, a startup focused on self-driving trucks. He resigned from Google on January 27. To a company racing to develop autonomous technology, that team's experience offered a shortcut through years of difficult research. And Uber was already interested.

**A01-P05**

That buyer was Uber. The acquisition would be described in headlines with a figure approaching $680 million. But the figure that matters first isn't the headline value. It's the initial cash actually paid—and the separate agreement about legal risk that came with the deal.

## A02 — THE $680 MILLION DEAL AND ITS HIDDEN RISK

**A02-P01**

In February 2016, Uber and Otto signed preliminary acquisition terms. Uber wanted engineers and the chance to speed up its self-driving efforts. Otto offered both. But buying a startup also meant confronting questions about what its newly recruited employees might still owe the employers they'd left behind.

**A02-P02**

The reported possible deal value reached approximately $680 million. That is a spectacular number for a young company. It is also easy to misunderstand. The Delaware court record described a structure involving future conditions and equity. The money delivered at the beginning was strikingly smaller.

**A02-P03**

Uber's Otto acquisition could have been worth up to $680 million. But the initial cash payment, according to Delaware court records, was only $100,000. Much of the potential value depended on conditions and future equity. So 'Uber handed Levandowski $680 million in cash' is the wrong story. The headline price wasn't the day-one payout.

**A02-P04**

Here's why that distinction matters. Uber was buying a possible future advantage. But one part of the acquisition concerned the past: potential claims tied to former Google employees. A big valuation wouldn't make those liabilities disappear. Somebody had to negotiate which risks might be covered and under what conditions.

**A02-P05**

By April 2016, the deal included a separate indemnification agreement dealing with certain legal liabilities. It was conditional; it wasn't a blank check. Years later, Levandowski would say Uber had to cover a personal judgment. Uber would dispute that. For now, the agreement was only one part of a deal that had not yet closed.

**A02-P06**

Before completing the purchase, Uber did something revealing: it hired outside digital-forensics specialists to examine whether former Google employees had retained confidential information. This investigation happened before the closing, not afterward. What did the specialists find? Enough to worry about—but not the simple answer either side wanted.

## A03 — THE INVESTIGATION BEFORE THE PURCHASE

**A03-P01**

The firm Uber hired was Stroz Friedberg. Its specialists examined digital devices and the possibility that former Google employees had retained sensitive materials. The timing is the important part: the review came after the preliminary acquisition terms but before Otto officially became part of Uber.

**A03-P02**

The inquiry raised concerns. Later, a shareholder lawsuit alleged that company lawyers had expressed reservations and that directors might not have received every warning. Those were the plaintiff's allegations about the acquisition process, not a court finding that the entire Uber board knowingly participated in stealing trade secrets.

**A03-P03**

Now the business decision had real stakes. A promising engineering team could help Uber compete faster. But a purchase might also import claims over confidential files. Due diligence couldn't give the board a guarantee about every future lawsuit. It could identify evidence and the limits of what investigators had actually established.

**A03-P04**

Uber investigated Google-data concerns before buying Otto. Its forensic firm, Stroz Friedberg, reported serious issues—but found no evidence that confidential Google information retained by Anthony Levandowski had been transferred to Otto or third parties. That didn't clear every possible violation. Uber nevertheless completed the acquisition. The warning existed; proof of that specific transfer did not.

**A03-P05**

Uber's board approved the deal. Otto became part of Uber in August 2016, and Levandowski entered its self-driving program. An important question remained unresolved: if the files had been copied, could anyone show that Uber's technology actually contained Google's protected work?

**A03-P06**

The next lead wasn't a new download or a confession. It came from technical material prepared for Uber, delivered somewhere it was never meant to go. A mundane supplier mistake was about to change the stakes.

## A04 — ONE EMAIL SENT TO THE WRONG COMPANY

**A04-P01**

December 2016. Google receives a supplier email that was supposed to go to Uber. Attached are circuit-board drawings connected to autonomous vehicles. According to later court accounts, Google saw similarities to its own designs. Suddenly, suspicions about what an engineer had copied were accompanied by a glimpse of engineering work intended for the buyer.

**A04-P02**

Google now had two different things to examine: the earlier record of copied files and technical drawings meant for Uber. They raised related questions, but neither alone traced those files into Uber's product. That evidentiary gap would matter when the accusations reached court.

**A04-P03**

One wrong email helped move a tech rivalry toward court. In December 2016, a supplier sent Google circuit-board drawings intended for Uber. Google suspected similarities to its designs. The email didn't prove Uber used Waymo's trade secrets—but it helped prompt further investigation. By February 2017, Waymo had filed a federal lawsuit against Uber and Otto.

**A04-P04**

Waymo, which grew out of Google's self-driving project, alleged theft and misuse of confidential technology, including LiDAR-related trade secrets. Uber denied incorporating Waymo's trade secrets into its own systems. These were opposing positions, and the courtroom had to decide what the evidence could support.

**A04-P05**

In May 2017, Judge William Alsup imposed limited preliminary restrictions, including measures affecting Levandowski's LiDAR work. That was not a final verdict proving every allegation. More importantly for the next turn, attention shifted to information that had once existed on physical storage devices—but could no longer be examined.

## A05 — FIVE DISKS AND THE MISSING VERDICT

**A05-P01**

Months before the acquisition closed, Levandowski told Uber executives about five disks he said held Google information. Uber later reported that he said the disks were destroyed and insisted it never received them. By the time this became a trial issue, nobody could independently inspect their contents.

**A05-P02**

The critical detail was when Uber learned of this claim, compared with when it disclosed that knowledge in litigation. The problem wasn't that missing disks automatically proved misappropriation. It was whether the delay could change how jurors assessed Uber's account.

**A05-P03**

Without the disks to examine, the judge faced a procedural question: could jurors treat Uber's late disclosure as a reason to doubt its position? The answer would shape the evidence presented at trial, even without proving what any disk had contained.

**A05-P04**

In January 2018, the judge permitted jurors to draw an unfavorable inference from Uber's delayed disclosure if they thought it warranted. They were not required to do so, and the order did not establish the missing disks' contents or prove that Uber used Waymo's trade secrets.

**A05-P05**

That prospect put credibility in play, not an automatic verdict. The parties could still argue over what the delay meant. It was a question jurors might have weighed—if the trial reached a decision.

**A05-P06**

In February 2018, the Waymo–Uber trial began. It looked as though a jury might finally answer what had been used and what could be proved. Instead, before the jury reached a verdict, the companies made a deal. Its headline value was massive. Its legal meaning was much narrower.

## A06 — THE $245 MILLION ENDING THAT WASN'T

**A06-P01**

On February 9, 2018, days after trial began, Waymo and Uber ended their corporate lawsuit. The dismissal with prejudice stopped those claims in that case before any jury verdict. But an agreement between those companies could not decide Levandowski's separate obligations to Google.

**A06-P02**

The settlement involved Uber shares then valued at roughly $245 million. It wasn't a criminal fine or damages calculated by jurors. Uber continued to deny incorporating Waymo's trade secrets into its technology. The lawsuit ended by negotiation, not by proving every allegation.

**A06-P03**

Uber's leadership expressed regret about how it handled the Otto acquisition. That did not turn the allegations into judicial findings. Waymo's case ended, but the settlement didn't cancel the former engineer's separate obligations to Google, and it didn't resolve the indemnification agreement Uber had signed while buying Otto.

**A06-P04**

Google's separate proceeding concerned Levandowski's duties as an employee, including disputes connected to recruiting colleagues. Unlike Waymo's lawsuit against Uber, it could impose liability on the engineer personally. That distinction would determine whose name appeared on the next enormous judgment.

**A06-P05**

As that employment dispute moved through arbitration, a second piece of the 2016 Otto deal became relevant: the agreement addressing certain legal liabilities. If the engineer faced a major personal award, would he argue that Uber had promised to cover it?

## A07 — A PERSONAL JUDGMENT THAT CAME BACK TO UBER

**A07-P01**

By 2020, Google's arbitration over Levandowski's employment and contractual duties had produced an award. The next step was confirmation in California court. That process—not a verdict finding Uber had used 14,000 files—would create the engineer's personal civil judgment.

**A07-P02**

On March 4, 2020, the court confirmed a civil judgment against Levandowski of $179,047,998.64. He filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy hours later. The judgment wasn't against Uber. But the bankruptcy would force a new confrontation over a contract signed while Uber was acquiring Otto.

**A07-P03**

There's an important second proceeding here. Federal prosecutors had charged Levandowski with 33 criminal counts over alleged theft or attempted theft of trade secrets. The eventual outcome was not 33 convictions. He pleaded guilty to one count concerning a specific confidential Google document—not a conviction for every file in the 14,000-file collection.

**A07-P04**

In August 2020, Levandowski was sentenced to 18 months in prison in that criminal case. In January 2021, he received a presidential pardon. That changed the federal criminal story; it did not erase the separate civil judgment already confirmed against him. The personal financial problem remained.

**A07-P05**

Why would any of this lead back to Uber? Because of the conditional indemnification agreement negotiated before the Otto acquisition. Levandowski argued that Uber should cover the liability connected to Google. Uber disputed whether the agreement required that coverage. The court hadn't simply ordered Uber to pay the $179 million.

**A07-P06**

So one question survived the corporation's 2018 settlement: who would bear the cost of the engineer's separate dispute with Google? And an even sharper question followed. Would the contract itself decide it—or would the parties have to negotiate another ending?

## A08 — WHY UBER PAID GOOGLE AGAIN

**A08-P01**

The bankruptcy brought Uber's 2016 indemnification promise into focus. Levandowski argued that it covered his Google liability; Uber disputed that reading. No court had simply ordered Uber to pay the personal award. The competing positions set up a different possible ending.

**A08-P02**

Rather than get a definitive court ruling enforcing the disputed indemnity, Google, Uber, and Levandowski reached a new global settlement in 2022. A negotiated resolution could end their fight without deciding whether the original 2016 promise had required Uber to cover the judgment.

**A08-P03**

That distinction matters because a settlement creates obligations of its own. The parties were not waiting for a jury to decide whether Uber had built with Google's files. They had negotiated how to resolve a separate liability dispute. One term would produce an unusual direct transfer.

**A08-P04**

In 2022, Uber paid Google again. The amount is sealed. This wasn't a secret Waymo jury verdict: court records connect the payment to a separate settlement involving Anthony Levandowski. Another $2 million was provided to fund his bankruptcy plan. Those were distinct payments—not a disclosed price for a second finding of stolen technology.

**A08-P05**

A later bankruptcy opinion made the legal basis explicit: Uber's main payment was made under the 2022 Global Settlement, not in performance of the disputed 2016 indemnification agreement. The court did not hold that Uber owed Levandowski's entire personal judgment under the older contract. The settlement resolved the dispute without that ruling.

**A08-P06**

The undisclosed payment amount prevents a reliable all-in price tag. But a later court wasn't trying to reveal that number. It confronted a different question: if Uber paid Google, could the transfer still count as income to Levandowski?

## A09 — THE COST THAT OUTLIVED THE DEAL

**A09-P01**

In 2025, a bankruptcy court answered that tax question. It held that Uber's main payment counted as gross income to Levandowski, even though Uber had paid Google directly. It did not calculate his final tax liability.

**A09-P02**

The dispute had moved a long way from the self-driving race. Three years after the settlement payment, a judge was still deciding how that transaction affected the engineer. A negotiated ending to one fight had created another question for the courts.

**A09-P03**

The court also said its tax decision should not be read as undermining the settlement's validity. And the opinion did not disclose the main payment's sealed amount. So the tax treatment received a ruling, while the precise size of Uber's second payment remained outside the public account.

**A09-P04**

The chain began with a download, then an acquisition reviewed by forensic investigators. When Levandowski faced a separate civil judgment, a disputed risk-sharing agreement from that acquisition came back into play. The courts documented those distinct steps; they never turned them into one verdict that Uber had used all the downloaded files.

**A09-P05**

Uber could end the public Waymo fight with stock and still pay Google under a different settlement years later. In 2025, a court was still addressing the consequences of that second payment. The record confirms it happened, but keeps the amount sealed. Signing the acquisition didn't settle who would ultimately bear the legal costs tied to the engineer's past.
