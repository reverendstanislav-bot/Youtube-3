## S01 — THE RULE THAT REVERSED ITSELF

Steam's contract said users could not combine their disputes.

Every claim had to go through arbitration one person at a time.

Then thousands of people did exactly that.

Valve removed arbitration from the agreement, tried to move pending cases into court, and asked a federal judge to stop them.

The judge refused to stop the arbitrations.

But that was not the end. The ruling set up an appeal over a question that sounds simple and is anything but: if a dispute begins under one contract, can the company rewrite the contract and change the forum while the dispute is still underway?

The answer matters far beyond Steam. It shows what can happen when a system designed to prevent one large case produces thousands of small ones instead.

And the strangest part is that the immediate legal fight is no longer about whether the original antitrust accusations are right.

It is about the rulebook.

## S02 — WHY STEAM HAD AN ARBITRATION CLAUSE

In 2012, Valve added an arbitration provision to the Steam Subscriber Agreement—the contract users accept to create a Steam account.

Arbitration moves a dispute out of ordinary court and puts it before a private neutral. Valve's agreement went further. Disputes had to be handled in individual binding arbitration through the American Arbitration Association, or AAA. Users could not bring a class action. They could not combine their cases without consent.

From a company's perspective, the structure is easy to understand. A class action can combine thousands or millions of people behind one complaint. Individual arbitration breaks that crowd apart.

One claimant. One file. One proceeding.

At least, that is the theory.

The agreement also said Valve could amend its terms. Users who did not accept an amendment could cancel their account or stop using the service.

For years, those provisions sat inside a contract most users probably encountered as a wall of text between them and their games.

Then the clause began to matter.

## S03 — THE ANTITRUST CLAIMS ENTER ARBITRATION

In 2021, a game publisher and two Steam users sued Valve. They alleged that Valve used market power and anticompetitive practices to inflate prices for games sold through Steam.

Those were allegations, not findings.

Valve invoked the Subscriber Agreement and moved to compel the consumer plaintiffs into arbitration. The court granted that request. The consumers went to the forum Valve's contract required. Publisher claims continued separately in court and eventually became part of a consolidated case known as *In re Valve Antitrust Litigation*.

That separate publisher case matters as background. But it is not the case at the center of this story.

The central story begins when individual Steam users start filing arbitration demands in large numbers.

## S04 / SH01 — THE CLAUSE THAT BACKFIRED

Steam's agreement did not allow users to combine claims into a class action. Every dispute had to proceed individually through arbitration.

That sounds like a system built to keep cases small.

But individual does not mean isolated.

Beginning in October 2023, Steam users represented by the same law firm filed separate arbitration demands alleging violations of federal antitrust law and Washington consumer law. The first group contained 997 demands. Thousands more followed in November and December.

Each demand remained an individual matter. But the administration, appointments, hearings and defense work could now repeat thousands of times.

The clause had blocked one large case.

It had not blocked large-scale pressure.

## S05 / SH02 — 4,991 INDIVIDUAL CLAIMS

The district court's May 2026 order recorded a striking number: at the cited stage, 4,991 claimants were pursuing arbitration against Valve before AAA.

That number did not mean 4,991 victories. It did not mean the claims had all been proven. It described the scale of the pending effort.

And scale changes the economics.

AAA's consumer mass-arbitration schedule uses an initiation stage, tiered per-case charges, appointment fees, final fees and separate arbitrator compensation. Not every case reaches every stage, and the precise amount depends on timing and administration.

So the honest takeaway is not a viral dollar figure.

It is the multiplication effect: one procedure, repeated thousands of times.

## S06 — THE NUMBER THAT DOES NOT SURVIVE FACT-CHECKING

Some versions of this story revolve around a claimed twenty-million-dollar fee crisis.

The available court order does not establish one verified global invoice, one payment obligation, or one final amount Valve was ordered to pay. AAA's published schedules changed over time. Cases moved through different stages. Arbitrator compensation was separate.

Without the invoices and payment record, the clean number is not defensible.

The verified story is already strong enough.

Valve's contract required individual arbitration. Thousands of people invoked it. And while those proceedings were moving, a new fight emerged over whether the arbitration provision itself could be enforced.

## S07 — THE FIRST CRACK IN THE OLD AGREEMENT

In May 2024, four claimants before one arbitrator asked to dismiss their own arbitrations, arguing that the arbitration agreement was unenforceable. In July, the arbitrator granted those requests so the claims could be pursued in court.

Those four people are not defendants in Valve's later injunction case. Their matters were folded into separate litigation.

Valve told the federal court that this arbitral ruling influenced what happened next.

And what happened next was a complete rewrite of the dispute system.

## S08 / SH03 — VALVE REMOVED ARBITRATION

On September 26, 2024, Valve announced an updated Steam Subscriber Agreement.

The company said it had eliminated the requirement that disputes be resolved through individual arbitration. It removed the class-action waiver. It removed the old cost and fee-shifting provisions. If support could not resolve a dispute, the new agreement directed it to court.

This was not a cosmetic edit.

For years, Valve had required users to leave court and arbitrate individually. Now the agreement pointed the other way.

But thousands of claims were already in the old forum.

Changing the route for future disputes was one thing.

Changing the route for pending disputes would become the real battle.

## S09 / SH04 — CAN A NEW CONTRACT STOP OLD CASES?

The updated agreement did not speak only about future disputes. Its court clause included claims that arose before the new agreement—or any prior agreement—and directed them to state or federal court in King County, Washington.

Valve's position was straightforward: users accepted the updated contract, the new contract superseded the old one, and the pending arbitrations no longer had an agreement behind them.

The defendants saw the same language in reverse. They had already filed under the contract Valve wrote, entered the forum Valve selected and spent time and money moving their cases forward.

Their position was that a later amendment could not pull away rights already exercised.

Which contract controls?

That question would move from a Steam pop-up to a federal appeal.

## S10 — HOW USERS WERE ASKED TO ACCEPT

According to evidence summarized by the district court, Valve sent email notices, displayed a pop-up inside the Steam client, published the agreement online and required acceptance before new purchases or Steam Wallet funding.

The notices said the update would take effect on November 1, 2024, unless the user deleted or discontinued use of the account before then.

Valve said 454 of the 572 later defendants affirmatively accepted through the pop-up, the purchase screen, or both. The remaining 118 did not click acceptance, but each logged in after the effective date. None deleted their accounts before then.

The defendants argued that this was not a meaningful choice because deleting an account could mean losing access to a purchased game library. They claimed average libraries worth more than three thousand dollars. Valve disputed that figure and argued users could stop using an account without losing already purchased licenses.

The number is contested. The undisputed structure is that Valve drafted the amendment and presented it to users who already had established digital libraries.
## S11 / SH05 — AAA DID NOT CLOSE THE CASES

One day after changing the agreement, Valve asked AAA to administratively close the pending arbitrations for lack of jurisdiction.

AAA declined.

That was not a final ruling that Valve was legally wrong. AAA said questions about arbitrability and which contract applied should be addressed by a court or by the merits arbitrators.

So the cases did not vanish with the contract update.

Some arbitrators concluded that the original agreement still controlled. Valve said many others stayed their proceedings rather than decide that question. Valve continued participating under a reservation of rights.

The outcomes were mixed.

When administrative closure failed, Valve turned to the forum named in its new agreement.

Federal court.

## S12 / SH06 — WHY 572 USERS BECAME DEFENDANTS

On October 18, 2024, Valve filed a federal action seeking to enjoin the arbitrations. The first filing named 624 respondents.

The court later struck that petition as procedurally improper and directed Valve to proceed through a civil complaint. On August 21, 2025, Valve filed that complaint against 572 defendants and moved for a preliminary injunction.

The roles had flipped.

Steam users who had brought individual claims in the forum Valve once required were now defendants in a lawsuit asking a judge to stop those proceedings.

By the May 2026 hearing, Valve said roughly four hundred remained active. The defendants disputed whether an exact count could be given.

This was not one global case ending one global way. Matters had been completed, withdrawn, stayed or otherwise resolved at different times.

## S13 — THE RESULTS WERE NOT ONE-SIDED

It is tempting to turn the record into a clean scoreboard.

The record refuses.

Defendants submitted evidence that eleven arbitrators had applied the original agreement in proceedings before them. Valve said many merits arbitrators stayed cases rather than authorize them to continue. Some completed arbitrations produced final awards for Valve. Other proceedings produced rulings and awards for claimants, including interim awards Valve sought to vacate.

That mixture matters.

The federal injunction request was not simply an appeal from one uniform arbitration result. It was an attempt to answer a contract question across hundreds of remaining disputes before more of them reached the end.

## S14 — WHAT VALVE ASKED THE JUDGE TO DO

A preliminary injunction is an emergency order issued before the entire case is finally decided. The party requesting it must clear demanding legal tests, including a sufficient likelihood of success and a showing of irreparable harm.

Valve argued that the updated agreement controlled and that being forced to arbitrate disputes without a current agreement to arbitrate caused irreparable injury.

The defendants answered that the old agreement controlled the cases already filed, that they had accrued rights under it, and that Valve could not use its amendment power to remove the forum after compelling users into it.

This was the convergence point.

The company that once went to court to enforce arbitration was now in court asking to stop arbitration.

And on May 27, 2026, the judge issued a thirty-two-page answer.

## S15 / SH07 — VALVE LOST THE MOTION, NOT THE CASE

The district court denied Valve's motion for a preliminary injunction.

The judge concluded that Valve was unlikely to succeed on its argument that the updated agreement could pull these pending disputes out of arbitration. At this stage, the court found the retroactive forum clause procedurally and substantively unconscionable as applied to these defendants.

But precision matters.

This was not a final judgment on the consumers' antitrust allegations. It did not validate every arbitration demand. It did not award one common sum. It decided whether Valve had shown enough to stop the proceedings before the larger contract case was resolved.

Valve lost that motion.

It did not receive the final answer.

And the same judge soon explained why the answer was still open.

## S16 — WHY THE COURT SAID NO

The order focused on sequence.

Valve wrote the arbitration clause, used it to compel consumers out of court, and participated in that forum while thousands spent time and money there. Only after those proceedings were underway did Valve amend the agreement and argue that the same disputes belonged back in court.

The judge treated that backward-looking change as one-sided. Users received no new benefit for losing the forum they had already entered, while Valve would shed the burden of arbitrations whose costs it had agreed to bear.

On harm, Valve argued it should not be forced to arbitrate without an agreement. The court answered that Valve had chosen the forum and participated for more than a year. The expense of continuing was monetary, not the kind of irreparable injury that automatically justified emergency relief.

Some defendants had also completed merits hearings or obtained favorable rulings or awards. Stopping everything could erase work already done and create timing problems for claims.

So the request was denied.

For a moment, that looks like the ending.

It is not.
## S17 — THE JUDGE CERTIFIES THE QUESTION

Valve asked the district court to allow an immediate, or interlocutory, appeal—a way for a higher court to resolve a controlling legal question before final judgment.

On July 30, the judge granted that request.

The order did not retreat from the earlier conclusion. The court still considered the retroactive clause likely unconscionable as applied here.

But the question was novel. Fair-minded judges could disagree. And if the Ninth Circuit agreed with Valve, there might be little left to decide about whether the updated agreement controlled the pending disputes.

The district case was stayed. Pending fights over awards and fees could be renewed later.

The dispute had moved upward.
## S18 / SH08 — THE NINTH CIRCUIT ENDING

The same judge who denied Valve's injunction also certified the contract question because reasonable jurists could disagree.

That distinction is the ending of this chapter.

On September 25, 2026, the Ninth Circuit opened appeal number 26-6269 under the name *Valve Corporation v. Fish and others*.

The public appellate record reviewed for this script contains no merits ruling.

The immediate question is not whether every antitrust allegation is true. It is whether Valve's updated agreement can govern disputes already pending under the old one.

Thousands of individual claims created the pressure.

One contract question now controls what happens next.
## S19 — WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS

The larger lesson is not that arbitration always favors companies or always favors consumers. Both claims are too simple.

A class-action waiver can prevent one combined case. But if each person can still file separately, coordination can recreate scale through thousands of initiation steps, appointments, hearings and deadlines.

The pressure changes shape.

Valve says the updated contract ended the obligation to arbitrate these matters. The defendants say the company cannot rewrite away a forum after claims are filed. The district court sided with the defendants only at the preliminary stage, then certified the issue for immediate appeal.

That is where the record ends.

Not with a final antitrust verdict.

Not with a verified twenty-million-dollar bill.

With a contract clause, written to control disputes, becoming the dispute that controls everything else.

The system was designed to keep claims apart. At scale, it brought the entire system into one fight.
