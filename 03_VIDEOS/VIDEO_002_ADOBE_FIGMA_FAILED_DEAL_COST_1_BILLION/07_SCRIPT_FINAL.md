## S01 — A BILLION DOLLARS FOR A DEAL THAT NEVER CLOSED

Adobe agreed to buy Figma for about twenty billion dollars.

The acquisition never closed.

Adobe still paid Figma one billion dollars.

That billion dollars was not a government fine.

It was not a court judgment.

And it was not part of the twenty-billion-dollar purchase price.

It was a contractual termination payment — money Adobe had agreed could become due if the deal failed under specified closing and regulatory circumstances.

The billion-dollar consequence was not invented after the acquisition collapsed.

It had been negotiated before the merger agreement was even signed.

So the real story is not simply why regulators had concerns about Adobe buying Figma.

It is how two companies priced the risk that the deal might never close — and how that risk eventually became a real one-billion-dollar cash payment.

---

## S02 — THE TWENTY-BILLION-DOLLAR BET

Figma had become a major collaborative design platform used by product teams.

Adobe was one of the world's largest creative-software companies, with products including Photoshop, Illustrator and Adobe XD.

On September fifteenth, twenty twenty-two, Adobe and Figma announced a definitive merger agreement.

The headline number was approximately twenty billion dollars.

The consideration was expected to be roughly half cash and half Adobe stock.

But the agreement did not guarantee that the transaction would close.

It still depended on regulatory approvals and other closing conditions.

And by the time the public heard the twenty-billion-dollar headline, the contract already contained another number that would later become just as important.

One billion dollars.

---

## S03 — THE CLAUSE EXISTED BEFORE THE DEAL

The negotiation record filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission shows how that number entered the deal.

On June nineteenth, twenty twenty-two, Adobe delivered a non-binding proposal to acquire Figma for twenty billion dollars, roughly half cash and half stock.

That proposal did not yet include a termination fee.

Figma did not negotiate only the headline price.

It also negotiated what would happen if the transaction could not close.

On July fifth, Figma proposed a reverse termination fee tied to failure to obtain required antitrust approvals.

Then, on July twentieth, Adobe came back with a revised proposal.

The twenty-billion-dollar price remained.

And now the proposal included a one-billion-dollar reverse termination fee.

That sequence matters.

June nineteenth: twenty billion dollars, no fee.

July fifth: Figma asks for protection against regulatory failure.

July twentieth: Adobe agrees to a one-billion-dollar reverse termination fee.

More than a year before the acquisition ended, the billion-dollar cost was already inside the deal architecture.

---

## S04 — WHAT A REVERSE TERMINATION FEE ACTUALLY DOES

A reverse termination fee is not a punishment imposed by the government.

It is a risk-allocation term negotiated by the parties to a transaction.

In simple terms, the buyer agrees that if specified closing failures occur, the seller can receive a defined payment.

In the Adobe-Figma agreement, the number was one billion dollars.

Section eight-point-two of the merger agreement set out the fee in defined antitrust and closing circumstances.

And the contract makes an unusually useful distinction for this story.

It says the termination fee is not a penalty.

It describes the payment as liquidated damages.

So the agreement did more than describe the deal the companies wanted to close.

It also described a financial consequence if that deal failed under the specified conditions.

The twenty-billion-dollar number described the upside.

The one-billion-dollar clause priced part of the downside.

## S05 — THEN THE DEAL BECAME PUBLIC

On September fifteenth, twenty twenty-two, the definitive agreement was signed and the transaction became public.

Adobe was proposing one of the largest software acquisitions of its era.

Figma would remain a separate company until closing, but the plan was for it to become part of Adobe.

At that moment, the story looked like a conventional giant technology acquisition.

A buyer.

A target.

A twenty-billion-dollar headline.

Regulatory approvals to obtain.

But there was already a one-billion-dollar failure mechanism sitting inside the contract.

The question was whether it would ever matter.

In twenty twenty-three, that question stopped being theoretical.

---

## S06 — THE REVIEWS EXPAND

The United Kingdom was one of the first places where the pressure became formal.

On May third, twenty twenty-three, the Competition and Markets Authority opened its inquiry into the proposed acquisition.

By June thirtieth, the CMA said there were grounds for deeper competition concerns.

On July thirteenth, the transaction was referred for an in-depth Phase Two investigation.

The European Commission received formal notification on June thirtieth.

On August seventh, it opened its own in-depth investigation.

And in the United States, the Justice Department's Antitrust Division was also investigating the transaction.

These were separate processes with different procedures.

The UK and EU reviews developed formal competition objections.

The public DOJ record for this story establishes an investigation, not a filed merger lawsuit.

The fee mattered because it had already been written into the companies' own contract, and the possibility of a failed closing was becoming harder to ignore.

---

## S07 — THE CONCERNS BECOME HARD TO IGNORE

By November twenty twenty-three, the UK and EU concerns had become much more explicit.

On November seventeenth, the European Commission adopted a Statement of Objections.

That was a formal statement of preliminary concerns, not a final prohibition decision.

Then, on November twenty-eighth, the UK CMA published provisional findings.

The CMA provisionally identified competition concerns involving all-in-one product design, vector editing and raster editing software.

The same day, it published a notice of possible remedies.

Those possible remedies included prohibition of the merger and structural divestitures.

The CMA document also discussed a possible divestiture of Figma Design and said that remedy would be substantially similar to prohibition while introducing additional risks.

The pressure was real.

But neither the CMA nor the European Commission had issued a final decision prohibiting the transaction.

The final legal "no" had not yet arrived.

---

## S08 — ADOBE AND FIGMA PUSH BACK

Adobe and Figma did not accept the regulators' theories as settled fact.

In their submissions to the CMA, the companies argued that their products and capabilities were complementary.

They argued that the transaction would be pro-competitive.

And they strongly disagreed with the provisional findings.

By December, the argument had shifted toward what kinds of remedies could realistically address the regulators' concerns.

On December fourteenth, Adobe and Figma submitted their response to the CMA's remedies notice.

In that response, the companies argued that the direction of the possible remedies effectively pointed toward either prohibition or divestiture of Figma Design.

That was the companies' characterization.

It was not a final CMA ruling.

But it captures how narrow they said the path had become.

No final UK or EU prohibition order had been issued.

Yet the practical question was now unavoidable.

Could this transaction still reach the approvals required to close?

---

## S09 — THE DEAL ENDS BEFORE THE FINAL “NO”

The final move came from Adobe and Figma themselves.

On December seventeenth, twenty twenty-three, the companies approved and signed a mutual termination agreement.

The next day, they announced that the acquisition was over.

Their public explanation was direct.

They said there was no clear path to obtaining the necessary regulatory approvals from the European Commission and the UK CMA.

That is different from saying a regulator had already issued a final prohibition order.

The companies ended the deal by mutual agreement before final UK and EU merits decisions arrived.

After the abandonment, the CMA cancelled its Phase Two inquiry.

The European Commission's investigation ended without a formal Commission decision on the merits.

And in the United States, the Justice Department's Antitrust Division publicly welcomed the abandonment and confirmed that it had investigated the transaction.

The public Stage One record does not show a DOJ merger lawsuit or a court judgment blocking the deal.

So the transaction did not end with a final courtroom loss.

It ended with a mutual termination after the companies said they no longer saw a clear path to the approvals they needed.

And that is the moment the billion-dollar clause stopped being theoretical.

## S10 — THREE DAYS LATER, THE CONTRACT FIRES

The mutual termination agreement did not just end the acquisition.

It specified what happened next.

Adobe was required to pay Figma one billion dollars in cash within three business days.

The agreement described that payment as liquidated damages.

It also made the payment Figma's sole and exclusive remedy under the merger agreement.

On December twentieth, twenty twenty-three, Adobe paid.

Adobe later disclosed that it used cash on hand.

Figma later disclosed that it received the same one-billion-dollar payment.

On December seventeenth, the companies ended the merger.

Three days later, the one-billion-dollar clause became cash.

The acquisition disappeared.

The termination fee did not.

And the payment did not come from a regulator's invoice.

It came from a contract the companies had negotiated themselves.

---

## S11 — THE TWO NUMBERS PEOPLE CONFUSE

This story has two giant numbers, and they mean completely different things.

About twenty billion dollars was the proposed acquisition consideration.

That was the amount associated with the deal Adobe wanted to close.

Because the acquisition never closed, Adobe did not pay Figma twenty billion dollars to buy the company.

One billion dollars was the actual termination payment.

Adobe paid it.

Figma received it.

Adobe later recorded the one-billion-dollar fee in operating expenses.

Figma recorded the payment in other income.

Those are separate categories.

Adobe did not spend twenty-one billion dollars on Figma.

One number described a purchase that never happened.

The other described the contractual payment that survived the failed deal.

That is the billion-dollar cost at the center of this story.

---

## S12 — FIGMA AFTER ADOBE

The failed acquisition did not end Figma's story.

Figma remained independent.

In July twenty twenty-five, the company filed to go public.

And on August first, twenty twenty-five, Figma completed its initial public offering.

The company sold twelve-point-five million of its own shares at thirty-three dollars per share.

Its later filing reported approximately three-hundred-ninety-three-point-one million dollars of net proceeds before offering costs.

That later success should not be turned into a causal claim the record does not support.

The public record does not establish that Adobe's one-billion-dollar termination payment caused Figma's IPO, its offering price, or its later market value.

What the record does establish is simpler.

Adobe did not acquire Figma.

Figma remained independent.

And less than two years after receiving the termination fee, it became a public company.

---

## S13 — WHAT THE CLAUSE WAS REALLY PRICING

At the beginning, Adobe and Figma negotiated the upside.

A roughly twenty-billion-dollar acquisition.

But they also negotiated the downside.

What would happen if the required regulatory clearance could not be obtained under the terms of the deal?

That risk became a one-billion-dollar reverse termination fee.

Then the regulatory pressure became real.

The UK CMA published provisional competition concerns and possible remedies.

The European Commission sent preliminary objections.

The Justice Department investigated.

Adobe and Figma disputed the regulators' analysis.

But by December twenty twenty-three, the companies said they no longer saw a clear path to the approvals they needed.

So they terminated the acquisition by mutual agreement.

And then the contract did exactly what it had been written to do.

Adobe paid Figma one billion dollars.

Not because a judge awarded it.

Not because a regulator fined Adobe.

And not because Adobe bought Figma.

The clearest billion-dollar consequence of the failed acquisition was a clause the two companies had negotiated before the regulatory fight really began.

That is what it cost.
