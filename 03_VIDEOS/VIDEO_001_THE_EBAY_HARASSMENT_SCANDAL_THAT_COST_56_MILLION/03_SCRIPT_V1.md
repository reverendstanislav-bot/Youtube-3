## S01 — A PACKAGE FROM EBAY

In August 2019, a couple in Massachusetts began receiving things that made no sense.

Live insects.

Threatening messages.

A bloody pig mask.

And eventually, people connected to the campaign were watching the couple in person and trying to put a GPS tracker on their car.

At first glance, this sounds like an internet stalking story.

But the people behind it were not anonymous trolls.

They worked in, or with, the security organization of eBay.

All seven former employees and contractors who were federally prosecuted over the campaign later pleaded guilty.

Years later, prosecutors filed criminal charges against eBay itself. The company entered a deferred prosecution agreement, admitted a detailed factual account, paid a three-million-dollar criminal penalty, and accepted an independent compliance monitor.

And in July 2026, the civil case finally ended in an announced settlement package worth fifty-five-point-seven million dollars.

But the strangest part is not what arrived at the front door.

It is how criticism of a company became a security problem inside that company — and how that decision kept getting more expensive long after the harassment stopped.

---

## S02 — TWO PUBLISHERS AND A VERY LARGE COMPANY

Ina and David Steiner ran EcommerceBytes, an independent publication covering online commerce.

They had been publishing since 1999.

Their business was small compared with eBay, but their subject was eBay's world: sellers, marketplaces, policy changes, executive decisions, and the complaints of people who depended on those platforms.

By the spring of 2019, a later federal court record shows that EcommerceBytes had become an increasing source of frustration for people inside eBay's leadership.

That distinction matters.

This was not a competitor trying to steal eBay's customers.

It was a publication criticizing the company and giving its readers a place to criticize it too.

And somewhere inside eBay, that reputational irritation started moving through the organization.

The question is how.

Because companies get criticized every day.

Most do not turn that criticism into a job for corporate security.

---

## S03 — THE TEMPERATURE RISES

The internal messages are where the story changes.

In April 2019, eBay communications executive Steve Wymer sent then-CEO Devin Wenig a message about an Ina Steiner article.

His words were: “We are going to crush this lady.”

Weeks later, according to the federal court record, Wenig told Wymer to “take her down.”

And on August 1, another message from Wenig said that if they were ever going to act, “now is the time.”

Those words are dramatic.

But they need to be handled carefully.

The civil court did not rule that these messages were direct orders to commit the crimes that followed.

In fact, the later court record says security chief Jim Baugh testified that Wenig never communicated directly with him about the Steiners. Wenig's relevant communications went through Wymer.

That legal distinction will matter later.

But inside the organization, the pressure was clearly intensifying.

Wymer's messages to Baugh became increasingly aggressive, including the phrase “Whatever it takes.”

At the same time, another executive relationship was pulling the issue toward security.

Wendy Jones was Baugh's direct supervisor. The court record says she told him to handle the issue “off the radar” and that she did not want details.

Again, that is not the same thing as a finding that Jones knew what the security team would later do.

But by this point, criticism from two publishers was no longer being discussed only as a communications problem.

It had entered the security chain.

---

## S04 — WHEN REPUTATION BECOMES SECURITY

Jim Baugh ran eBay's security department.

And before the most notorious phase of the campaign, members of that security operation had already traveled to Massachusetts.

During an early trip, one member of the team surveilled the Steiners and spray-painted a name connected to their publication on their fence.

Baugh later described the trip to Wymer as a “tap on the shoulder.”

That moment is important because it shows how the problem was being translated.

From the top, there was anger about criticism.

In the middle, there were increasingly forceful messages.

At the security level, those messages were becoming action.

Then, in early August 2019, the process became more organized.

On August 6, Baugh held meetings with security-team members to plan an operation against the Steiners.

The plan included unwanted deliveries and online harassment.

The next day, Baugh met with Wymer.

And then the operation accelerated.

---

## S05 — THE OPERATION

The campaign used shock because shock was the point.

The Steiners received threatening online messages and disturbing deliveries.

The admitted record includes live insects and a bloody pig mask.

There were also Craigslist posts designed to send strangers toward the Steiners and online messages intended to frighten them.

The details are bizarre enough that they can make the story feel almost absurd.

But focusing only on the objects misses the more important fact.

This was organized.

People were planning activities, traveling, communicating, and using corporate-security personnel and resources around a campaign directed at two private citizens.

And then the line moved again.

On August 15, Baugh, David Harville and Veronica Zea traveled to the Steiners' area.

According to the federal court record, they attempted to install a GPS tracker on the Steiners' vehicle.

They also surveilled and followed the couple.

The tracker was not established as successfully installed.

But the attempt changed the nature of the story.

This was no longer simply anonymous online harassment or disturbing deliveries.

It had become a physical surveillance operation.

And the next day, the Steiners went to the police.

---

## S06 — THE SECOND PROBLEM

Natick police began investigating on August 16, 2019.

That should have ended the operation.

Instead, it created a second problem.

The first problem was what the security team had done.

The second was what members of that team did once investigators started asking questions.

The record admitted by eBay and described by federal prosecutors includes false statements, deletion of digital evidence, and falsified records.

In other words, a security operation designed to deal with a reputational problem had now created an obstruction problem too.

eBay began its own internal investigation days later.

By the end of August, several security employees had been placed on leave. Over time, implicated employees were terminated and one contractor's relationship ended.

The company was now trying to contain a crisis created by the very function that existed to contain risk.

But internal discipline was only the beginning.

Federal prosecutors were coming next.

---

## S07 — SEVEN GUILTY PLEAS

Beginning in 2020, federal prosecutors brought criminal cases against people involved in the campaign.

Eventually, all seven former eBay employees and contractors who were prosecuted pleaded guilty.

That point is important because a guilty plea is not an allegation.

It establishes criminal guilt without a trial.

Jim Baugh, the former security chief, received a sentence of fifty-seven months in prison.

David Harville received twenty-four months.

Other participants received combinations of prison, probation, home confinement, and financial penalties.

By 2024, the individual criminal cases had produced a clear story of personal accountability.

And from a distance, that can look like the ending.

A group of employees committed crimes.

The company discovered what happened.

The employees were fired.

They pleaded guilty.

Case closed.

Except it was not.

Because federal prosecutors eventually turned from the people inside the company to the company itself.

---

## S08 — EBAY GETS ITS OWN CRIMINAL CASE

In January 2024, prosecutors filed a six-count criminal Information against eBay.

The counts included interstate stalking, stalking through electronic communications, witness tampering, and destruction or falsification of records connected to a federal investigation.

But eBay was not convicted on those charges.

Instead, the company entered a deferred prosecution agreement.

A deferred prosecution agreement works differently from a conviction.

The government files charges, but prosecution is held back while the company complies with negotiated conditions. If the agreement is completed successfully, the criminal case is intended to end without a trial on those corporate charges.

For eBay, the agreement came with real consequences.

The company admitted a detailed factual account of the conduct.

It agreed to pay a three-million-dollar criminal penalty.

And it accepted an independent compliance monitor for three years, along with cooperation and compliance obligations.

That is the moment this story stops being explainable as only a handful of employees going rogue.

The federal resolution attached consequences directly to eBay as a corporation.

But it still did not answer the hardest question.

How far up the organization did responsibility for the campaign extend?

For that, the story moves back to a civil lawsuit filed by the Steiners.

---

## S09 — HOW HIGH DID RESPONSIBILITY GO?

The Steiners filed their federal civil case in July 2021.

Their lawsuit named eBay and multiple individuals and entities.

A complaint can allege many things.

That does not mean a court has found all of them true.

The more useful moment comes four years later.

In August 2025, Judge Patti Saris issued a major summary-judgment order.

Summary judgment is one of the points where a court asks whether a claim has enough evidence to go to a jury.

If a judge denies summary judgment, that does not mean the defendant has lost.

It means the issue is not being removed before trial.

And the court did not treat every defendant or every theory the same way.

A security contractor named PFC won summary judgment on the claims against it.

But significant claims involving eBay and former executives survived.

For Devin Wenig, the court record included an important limitation: Baugh testified that Wenig had not communicated with him directly about the Steiners.

At the same time, the judge held that, viewing the evidence in the Steiners' favor as required at that stage, a jury could potentially find Wenig's encouragement was a substantial factor in what followed.

For Steve Wymer, the court pointed to a much more extensive communication record and held that a reasonable jury could find he had induced, encouraged or authorized tortious conduct.

Those are serious rulings.

But they are still not final findings of liability.

And the executive defendants were not being convicted of crimes in this civil case.

There was another counterweight too.

According to reporting on eBay's internal investigation, the company's lawyers had said they found no evidence that Wenig or Wymer directed the criminal acts or knew those acts would follow.

So by late 2025, the case had arrived at a much more complicated question than the criminal prosecutions.

The conduct of the security team was no longer seriously in doubt.

The unresolved question was how responsibility should be allocated higher up the organization.

A jury was getting close to answering that.

Then the case appeared to end.

---

## S10 — THE SETTLEMENT THAT DIDN'T SETTLE IT

On February 25, 2026, just before trial, the parties reported a settlement in principle.

The terms were not disclosed.

The case was conditionally dismissed while the parties worked to finalize the agreement.

After years of criminal cases, a corporate DPA, civil discovery, and pretrial rulings, it looked like the final chapter.

Then the deal failed.

By June, the settlement had not been finalized.

The civil case was reopened.

A new trial date was set for January 2027.

That reversal mattered because it put the biggest unresolved issue back on the table.

If the case went to trial, a jury could be asked to decide civil responsibility for conduct that had already generated guilty pleas and a corporate federal resolution.

But before that trial happened, the parties reached another deal.

This one became the final settlement.

And this time, the numbers were public.

---

## S11 — WHAT IT COST

The final agreement was announced in late July 2026.

The total announced settlement package was fifty-five-point-seven million dollars.

But that number needs to be broken apart.

It was not a fifty-five-point-seven-million-dollar judgment.

And it was not a fifty-five-point-seven-million-dollar payment made only by eBay.

According to the announced terms, forty-eight-point-seven million dollars was compensation to the Steiners.

Another seven million dollars consisted of charitable commitments.

Of the direct compensation, eBay's announced share was forty-six-point-one-five million dollars.

Devin Wenig's was two million.

Wendy Jones's was five hundred thousand.

Steve Wymer's was fifty thousand.

Separately, eBay committed six million dollars to nonprofit organizations, and Wenig committed one million dollars to a First Amendment-focused charity in Ina Steiner's name.

That is how the total reached fifty-five-point-seven million.

And it is important not to combine that number with eBay's separate three-million-dollar criminal penalty and pretend there was one giant fifty-eight-point-seven-million-dollar award.

There wasn't.

They were different legal resolutions, at different times, serving different purposes.

One was a federal criminal resolution with the company.

The other was the final civil settlement package.

That distinction makes the ending more complicated.

It also makes it more revealing.

Because the real cost of the campaign did not arrive in one courtroom on one day.

It accumulated.

---

## S12 — THE COST OF TURNING CRITICISM INTO AN OPERATION

In its July 2026 statement, eBay apologized to Ina and David Steiner.

The company called the conduct of the former employees wrong and reprehensible.

It also acknowledged what it described as unprofessional communications involving Wenig, Wymer and Jones.

But the settlement meant there would be no jury verdict deciding the surviving civil liability questions against those former executives.

So the cleanest ending is not to pretend the court answered every question.

It did not.

What the public record does show is enough.

A small publication criticized a much larger company.

Inside that company, the criticism generated escalating internal hostility.

The issue moved into corporate security.

Security personnel planned and carried out a campaign of harassment and surveillance.

When police began investigating, the problem expanded again into obstruction.

Seven prosecuted participants pleaded guilty.

eBay itself later entered a federal deferred prosecution agreement, admitted detailed facts, paid a three-million-dollar criminal penalty, and accepted an independent monitor.

Then years of civil litigation pushed unresolved questions about responsibility higher into the organization.

The first 2026 settlement failed.

The second ended the case with a fifty-five-point-seven-million-dollar settlement package.

There were personal costs: guilty pleas and prison sentences.

There were corporate legal costs: federal charges, a DPA, a penalty, and years of monitoring.

And there were civil financial costs measured in tens of millions of dollars.

All of that grew out of a problem that began with something every major company eventually faces:

criticism.

The expensive decision was not that the criticism existed.

It was what people inside the company decided to do about it.

And that is what it cost.
