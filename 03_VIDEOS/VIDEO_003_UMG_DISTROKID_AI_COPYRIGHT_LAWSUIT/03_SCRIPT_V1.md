## S01 — 4,562 TRACKS AND ONE UNEXPECTED DEFENDANT

Universal Music says one account name released **4,562 tracks through DistroKid in twelve months**.

That number comes from a lawsuit filed by UMG Recordings, Capitol Records and Capitol CMG. It is an allegation in a complaint, not a court finding.

The companies filed the case on September fifteenth, twenty twenty-six, in federal court in Delaware.

And the most important defendant is not an AI music generator. It is not Spotify. It is not the account name behind those 4,562 tracks.

It is DistroKid — the company in the middle, the distributor that takes music and metadata from uploaders and sends it toward streaming platforms.

That changes the question.

This is not only a fight over whether particular recordings infringed somebody's rights.

It is a fight over what responsibility belongs to the pipe that allegedly moved disputed material into the streaming ecosystem — especially once that pipe was allegedly told there was a problem.

UMG's complaint goes much further than one account and one giant number. It accuses DistroKid and related entities of copyright infringement and deceptive trade practices, and it says the disputed material included altered copies, reused artwork, unauthorized samples and other recordings that UMG says incorporated protected works.

DistroKid strongly disputes Universal's allegations.

And as of our October third research refresh, we identified no merits ruling, no damages award and no injunction deciding who is right.

So this story begins with a huge allegation.

But it is really about a much less visible piece of the music business: the distributor between the person who uploads a track and the platform where the public hears it.

---

## S02 — WHAT DISTROKID ACTUALLY DOES

DistroKid is not Spotify, and it is not a record label. It is the pipe in between.

An artist or uploader provides the audio file and the metadata. DistroKid then delivers that material to streaming and social platforms.

On its current website, DistroKid markets unlimited uploads and distribution to more than 150 destinations, including services such as Spotify, Apple Music and TikTok.

That promise is a huge part of why digital distribution changed independent music.

A creator no longer needs a traditional label relationship just to get a release delivered around the world.

The process is designed to remove friction.

And that is exactly why the role of the distributor matters in this lawsuit.

If a business is built to move enormous volumes of music quickly, what happens when a rights conflict appears inside that flow?

The answer is not automatically that the distributor is liable for whatever an uploader does.

And the existence of a bad upload does not, by itself, tell us what the distributor knew, what it did, or what legal duty the court will ultimately apply.

But it gives us the structure of the dispute.

Uploader on one side.

Streaming platform on the other.

And in the middle, a distributor handling both the file and the information attached to it.

UMG's case is trying to make that middle layer the center of the story.

## S03 — THIS IS NOT SIMPLY “AI MUSIC IS ILLEGAL”

The most important sentence in Universal's complaint may be the one explaining what this lawsuit is **not** about.

UMG says the case is not about AI-generated music when that music is clearly disclosed as AI-generated.

That matters because the fastest version of this story is also the most misleading one: “Universal sued DistroKid because AI music is illegal.”

That is not what the complaint says.

The complaint instead advances allegations of copyright infringement and deceptive trade practices.

AI-generated or mass-generated content is part of the factual story UMG tells, but the legal theory is not a blanket demand to outlaw every song created with AI tools.

The real fight is about alleged infringement, alleged deception, and what UMG says DistroKid did as the distributor in the middle.

And none of those allegations has been established by a merits ruling.

DistroKid's own current policy makes the distinction even clearer.

Its help center says music made with AI tools can be distributed if the uploader owns the rights, does not impersonate someone without permission, does not mass-generate spam and does not infringe.

That is DistroKid's stated policy. It is not proof that every upload in this case followed those rules.

But it shows why the interesting question is not “AI or no AI.”

The interesting question is what passed through the distribution system, what information traveled with it, what warnings allegedly appeared, and what happened next.

## S04 — 1,000 EXAMPLES ARE NOT 1,000 COURT FINDINGS

UMG's complaint points to **1,000 examples** in its exhibits and says its investigation had identified **nearly 2,000 allegedly infringing DistroKid-distributed recordings** by the time it filed.

Those are big numbers.

They are also plaintiffs' assertions, not 1,000 or 2,000 court findings.

That distinction is essential because a complaint is one side's case at the beginning of litigation.

It tells us what UMG says happened and what it intends to prove.

It does not tell us what a judge or jury will eventually accept.

So the numbers show the scale of Universal's theory — not the outcome of the case.

The complaint alleges several different kinds of disputed material.

UMG describes speed-altered or remixed copies, recordings that allegedly reused protected artwork, instrumentals paired with substituted vocals, unlicensed samples, and longer compilations that allegedly incorporated protected recordings.

The details matter because they do not all present the same factual question.

A copied recording, a sample, a piece of artwork and a disputed metadata identifier can involve different evidence and different legal arguments.

For the film, the point is not to declare every item infringing.

The point is that UMG is presenting a scale argument.

It is saying this was not an isolated upload that slipped through once.

DistroKid says Universal's allegations are wrong.

The court has not decided between those positions.

## S05 — THE TINY CODE BEHIND A RECORDING

One of the strangest parts of the lawsuit is a code most listeners never see: the **ISRC**.

The International Standard Recording Code is used to identify a specific sound recording or music-video recording.

Official ISRC guidance describes it as a unique and permanent identifier for that recording.

So when music moves through a distribution system, the distributor is not moving only an audio file.

It is also moving metadata that tells industry systems which recording they are dealing with.

UMG's complaint includes allegations it describes as “ISRC theft” or misuse of recording identifiers.

That is an allegation, not a finding that DistroKid or any uploader committed theft.

And a metadata conflict by itself does not prove copyright infringement.

But it exposes something important about the plumbing of digital music.

To a listener, two files may look like nothing more than two tracks on a screen.

To a platform, the metadata attached to those files helps identify the recording, connect information across systems, and distinguish one release from another.

That means a dispute about a tiny code can become part of a much larger fight about ownership, attribution and distribution.

And once you understand that, the next part of UMG's theory becomes much more important.

Because the lawsuit does not stop at what allegedly entered the system.

It focuses heavily on what UMG says happened after rights conflicts were detected.

---

## S06 — WHY A RIGHTS NOTICE CHANGES THE STORY

The lawsuit gets more serious at the moment UMG says DistroKid was told there was a rights conflict.

The complaint describes rights-management systems that can flag a conflict between an uploaded recording and material claimed by a rightsholder.

UMG alleges DistroKid received notices about some disputed recordings.

And UMG further alleges that, in some instances, distribution continued on other services even after DistroKid had acknowledged a rights conflict.

That post-notice conduct is Universal's allegation.

It has not been adjudicated.

But narratively, it changes the question from “How did a bad upload get through?” to “What happened after the distributor was told there might be a problem?”

That is the hidden mechanism at the center of the film.

An uploader can make a false claim.

A file can be mislabeled.

A screening system can miss something.

None of those facts automatically answers whether a distributor is legally responsible.

But notice creates a record that can become important if UMG can prove the underlying facts and then show that DistroKid's own conduct crossed whatever legal line applies.

That is why the middle layer matters.

The allegation is no longer just that questionable material existed somewhere on the internet.

It is that a company whose business is distributing music allegedly received information about specific rights conflicts and, according to UMG, did not always stop the distribution that mattered.

DistroKid disputes that story.

And the court has not yet decided it.

## S07 — DISTROKID SAYS UNIVERSAL HAS IT WRONG

DistroKid says Universal's version of the story is wrong.

In its public response, the company says it strongly disagrees with UMG's allegations and intends to defend itself vigorously.

DistroKid says it takes copyright protection, fraud prevention and the integrity of the music ecosystem seriously.

It also says it continuously invests in protections.

And its published AI-upload rules require users to own the rights to what they distribute, avoid unauthorized impersonation, avoid mass-generated spam and avoid infringement.

Those are meaningful pieces of DistroKid's position.

They are not the same thing as a court ruling that every disputed upload complied with those rules.

A written policy tells us what a company says its system requires.

The lawsuit asks what actually happened in the situations UMG describes.

That difference is where the conflict lives.

Universal's complaint paints a picture of scale, repeated disputes, metadata problems and notice.

DistroKid responds that it has safeguards, takes these problems seriously and rejects Universal's allegations.

At this stage, both positions need to stay on screen.

Because there is no verdict to use as a shortcut.

The documentary can explain why UMG thinks the distributor layer matters.

It can also explain why DistroKid says the accusation does not fairly describe its practices.

What it cannot honestly do is pretend the unresolved parts have already been decided.

## S08 — WHY $150,000 PER WORK IS NOT THE VALUE OF THE LAWSUIT

You can create a fake nine-figure headline from this lawsuit with one multiplication.

That would be misleading.

UMG alleges willful infringement and asks for statutory damages up to **$150,000 per qualifying work where legally applicable**.

That number is a requested statutory ceiling, not a verdict.

There is no damages award in the record we reviewed.

And the complaint does not state one fixed aggregate dollar value for the case.

Liability has not been established, and neither has willfulness.

So multiplying $150,000 by 1,000 examples, or by nearly 2,000 alleged recordings, would manufacture a number the court has never ordered and the complaint does not plead as a fixed total.

The real stakes are large enough without fake math.

UMG also seeks injunctive relief and other remedies, and it can pursue actual damages and profits as an alternative to statutory damages under the theories it pleads.

Those remedies matter because the business consequence could be broader than a check.

A court fight over distributor conduct can affect how aggressively accounts are screened, how quickly disputed material is removed, how repeat problems are handled, and how platforms evaluate the distributors feeding them content.

Those are potential consequences, not court-ordered changes today.

The case is still at an early stage.

So the honest number to remember is not a fictional total.

It is the fact that Universal is asking the court to put potentially expensive legal consequences on conduct it says happened inside the distribution layer.

## S09 — THE TIMING AROUND THE FIGHT

The lawsuit arrived during an unusually sensitive moment for the digital-distribution business.

On July sixth, twenty twenty-six, CVC announced a definitive agreement to make a majority investment in DistroKid.

Insight Partners was expected to retain a significant minority position, and DistroKid president Phil Bauer was expected to continue leading the company.

The official CVC announcement did not disclose the transaction terms.

That matters because reported valuation numbers should not be turned into an official purchase price.

Then, on September fourteenth — one day before UMG filed its complaint — IFPI launched the Streaming Integrity Initiative.

The initiative described baseline anti-fraud practices such as verification, vetting, action, information sharing and measurement.

UMG was listed among the initial supporters.

DistroKid was not named in that initial supporter list.

That absence does not prove DistroKid rejected the initiative, violated it, or did anything wrong.

And there is no basis in our source record to say the CVC transaction caused the lawsuit or that the IFPI initiative was created to target DistroKid.

These events matter as context.

They show a music industry already focused intensely on streaming fraud, identity, verification and the integrity of the supply chain just as this lawsuit reached federal court.

The filing date is September fifteenth, twenty twenty-six.

The first public docket snapshot listed October sixth as the answer date for the DistroKid defendants.

But that is no longer the safe deadline to repeat.

On September twenty-eighth, a later docket entry shows the court reset the answer deadline and established an open, answer, reply and surreply briefing schedule.

The accessible docket text in our Stage One source pack does not expose the new dates inside that order.

That is a dated source limitation, not proof that no filing could exist somewhere we have not accessed.

More important, we identified no merits ruling, no damages award and no injunction.

That means almost every dramatic factual claim in the complaint still has to be treated as an allegation.

The case is not at the ending.

It is near the beginning.

UMG has filed a serious case.

DistroKid says the allegations are wrong.

## S11 — WHAT UNIVERSAL IS REALLY ASKING THE COURT TO TEST

Strip away the AI headlines, the giant upload counts and the damages number, and the business question becomes easier to see.

What responsibility can attach to a distributor that makes it easy to send music at scale to major platforms when allegedly infringing recordings, metadata conflicts or rights notices are said to pass through its system?

There are three different actors in that chain.

The uploader creates the submission and makes claims about the material.

The distributor accepts the file and metadata and sends the release onward.

The platforms receive that release and may operate their own rights-management or detection systems.

A clean story has to keep those roles separate.

UMG is not asking the court to decide that every distributor is automatically responsible for every bad upload.

Its complaint is built around the specific conduct it alleges here: the material it says was distributed, the identifiers and metadata it challenges, the notices it says DistroKid received, and the actions it says followed.

DistroKid disputes those allegations and points to its safeguards and policies.

So the fight is not really about whether digital distribution should exist.

Digital distribution is now basic infrastructure for independent music.

The fight is about what happens when infrastructure designed for speed and scale collides with copyright ownership, fraud controls and notice.

That is a much bigger business question than whether one strange track should have appeared on one streaming service.

---

## S12 — THE PIPE IS NOW THE BATTLEGROUND

Digital distribution made global music release dramatically easier.

A musician can create a recording, upload a file, attach metadata and reach major platforms without building the old label-and-distributor machinery that once stood in the way.

DistroKid built its business around making that process fast and accessible.

UMG now alleges that the same distribution pipe carried infringing and misleading material at meaningful scale and that rights conflicts did not always stop the conduct it challenges.

DistroKid rejects that account.

It says it takes copyright and fraud prevention seriously, maintains rules against infringement and spam, and will defend itself.

As of October third, the court had not decided which version of the disputed facts or which legal theory will prevail.

So there is no honest winner to announce.

There is only a system that has become important enough to fight over.

The most important question in this case is not whether AI can make a song.

It is what a distributor has to do when the system between creator and platform is told that a song may not belong there.

If UMG can prove the facts and legal theories in its complaint, the case could put new pressure on how distributors handle verification, metadata, repeat problems and rights notices.

If DistroKid defeats those theories, that would matter too — because it would define limits on how far responsibility for an uploader's conduct can be pushed onto the middleman.

Those outcomes are still unresolved.

For now, the lawsuit has already made one thing visible.

The invisible pipe between creator and platform is no longer just infrastructure.

It is the battleground.
