### A01 — FROM PARTNERS TO HARDWARE RIVALS

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S001 S017*

**A01-P01 · SHORT SH02 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

In twenty twenty-four, Apple and OpenAI announced a partnership bringing ChatGPT to Siri. Two years later Apple sued OpenAI over alleged hardware secrets. OpenAI denied wrongdoing. The companies could collaborate on software while competing in physical AI devices. The real dispute is whether any protected company information crossed the line with employees changing jobs.

**A01-P02**

Imagine an engineer who knows which coating survives a thousand tests, which supplier can manufacture a difficult part, or why one battery design failed. That person can take their experience to a new job. The question is whether they can also take private files, drawings, and instructions belonging to the old employer.

**A01-P03**

And here, Apple didn't simply accuse a rival of hiring its engineers. It said the line between a person's expertise and Apple's confidential material had been crossed. To understand that accusation, we need to meet the people named in its complaint.

### A02 — THE LAWSUIT APPLE FILED

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S001*

**A02-P01**

On July tenth, twenty twenty-six, Apple filed a civil lawsuit in federal court in San Jose. The defendants included two former Apple employees, Chang Liu and Tang Yew Tan, together with OpenAI entities and io Products, a business connected to OpenAI's consumer hardware effort.

**A02-P02**

Apple's complaint made six civil claims: four alleging theft of trade secrets under federal law and two about employee intellectual-property agreements. These weren't criminal charges or findings of guilt. Apple still had to prove its version of the events, and the defendants disputed it.

**A02-P03**

Apple's theory focused on more than a single folder. In its telling, recruiting interviews, access to old systems, and specific knowledge about future devices all pointed toward a larger pattern. Apple wanted the court to stop alleged misuse and order the return or protection of information it considered confidential.

**A02-P04**

It also sought financial remedies. But the complaint did not establish a specific dollar amount that OpenAI had already cost Apple. A demand for damages is not the same thing as a damages award.

### A03 — THE DOZENS OF APPLE FILES

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S001 S002*

**A03-P01 · SHORT SH03 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

Apple's complaint describes a rare access problem after engineer Chang Liu left the company. Apple alleges that he used it to reach private hardware material and download dozens of files. Dozens — not the one hundred-plus OpenAI would describe later. And at this point, these were Apple's allegations, not a judge's findings.

**A03-P02**

That difference in the numbers matters. The first group is Apple-origin material that Apple says Liu accessed. The second group, which we will reach later, concerns different OpenAI-origin documents and an unnamed employee moving in the opposite direction. They are not one giant collection of stolen files.

**A03-P03**

Apple said Liu retained an Apple work laptop after leaving, used another computer to reach internal information, and advised a colleague considering an OpenAI position on how to avoid attention. Those were contested claims. The forensic record and the context around communications mattered more than the accusations alone.

**A03-P04**

There's a second question hiding inside the first. Even if a file was downloaded, where did it go? Was it opened at the new employer? Did it contain a legally protected secret? Was it used to design a competing product? Those are separate steps. A dramatic screenshot of a download count cannot answer all of them.

### A04 — OPENAI'S DEFENSE AND THE FORENSIC FIGHT

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S002 S003 S004 S007 S005*

**A04-P01 · SHORT SH04 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

OpenAI offers a very different account of Chang Liu's access to Apple systems. It says access sometimes remained active after employees left and that Apple colleagues even asked Liu for help. That is OpenAI's defense, not a court finding. The difference matters because access, permission, and misuse are three separate questions.

**A04-P02**

OpenAI posted selected messages that it said showed Apple colleagues requesting Liu's help after his departure. The company used them to support its defense that Apple had not properly shut off access. Selected excerpts, however, are party-provided material, not a complete independent reconstruction.

**A04-P03**

The battle became more technical. The parties disagreed over what forensic material showed about the use of files, AI tools, and possible deletion of information. The defense rejected Apple's interpretation of key findings and questioned whether evidence showed that a contested file was used in the way Apple suggested.

**A04-P04**

In August, the defendants moved to dismiss the case. They also opposed Apple's request for an early injunction. But a motion to dismiss is just a request. An opposition brief is just one side's argument. Neither filing by itself ends a lawsuit.

### A05 — THE INTERVIEWS AND THE PHYSICAL PARTS

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S001 S002*

**A05-P01**

There was another former Apple employee at the center of the case: Tang Yew Tan, a veteran of more than two decades at Apple. Apple alleged that sensitive knowledge about its hardware programs came up around OpenAI's efforts to hire people from the company.

**A05-P02**

One message described in Apple's complaint contained a remarkable phrase: actual parts. Apple alleged a recruiting candidate was asked to bring physical hardware from work to an OpenAI interview. In the complaint, a candidate expressed surprise at that idea. What was requested is not the same as what was actually handed over.

**A05-P03 · SHORT SH05 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

Apple alleges that confidential hardware information and even physical parts were discussed during OpenAI recruiting. The employee at the center of that allegation was former Apple executive Tang Yew Tan. But asking about a part is not proof that anyone delivered it. And Apple still had to establish which information was protected and how it was used.

**A05-P04**

Apple's complaint quoted a request for 'Actual parts' in a recruiting discussion. One candidate, Apple said, reacted with surprise. That makes the interview guides consequential: what was asked, what was actually provided, and what was recorded are not the same fact.

**A05-P05**

That is why the actual interview records became so important. If the sessions were ordinary assessments of professional experience, the notes would support one picture. If the documents recorded requests for internal product information, Apple would argue they supported another. The contents and circumstances mattered far more than a dramatic accusation in a headline.

**A05-P06**

And now the case reached a point where the judge would decide how much of those records Apple could see early.

### A06 — WHAT THE JUDGE REALLY ORDERED

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S005*

**A06-P01**

On October thirteenth, Judge Edward Davila issued a ten-page order. Apple had asked for expedited discovery — which means access to certain records earlier than the ordinary timetable. It wanted evidence it believed could help evaluate its separate request for a preliminary injunction.

**A06-P02**

The court ordered certain forensic images already collected to be produced quickly, with defined time frames for other materials. It also authorized limited testimony from Chang Liu, Tang Tan, Alyssa Peng and a corporate representative about specified interview and employment subjects. No one had to infer that the court had settled disputed file ownership.

**A06-P03 · SHORT SH06 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

On October thirteenth, a federal judge gave Apple only part of its request for early evidence from OpenAI. Some forensic records, interview materials, and limited depositions were allowed. Broad demands for research-and-development documents were not accelerated, and other requests were deferred. This was a discovery ruling — not a verdict that OpenAI stole Apple's secrets.

**A06-P04**

Some of Apple's more sweeping early demands about research into metal finishes, batteries, and power management did not receive the accelerated treatment Apple wanted. Other requests depended on a phrase the judge found too vague: Apple-originated information. The court required a more tailored definition before deciding how far those requests could reach.

**A06-P05**

The judge also declined, in that discovery decision, to resolve the opposing forensic experts' arguments about whether evidence had been destroyed. The order did not decide the merits of Apple's theft allegations, and it was not a ruling granting Apple's separate preliminary injunction request.

**A06-P06**

Still, it changed the practical situation. The companies were moving beyond public statements and into narrower categories of records that had to be produced. Questions about who interviewed whom, what guides were used, and what had been collected would be tested against documents, not just press releases.

**A06-P07**

Then, before the next day was over, the direction of the story changed.

### A07 — THE HUNDRED FILES, IN THE OTHER DIRECTION

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S006*

**A07-P01**

On October fourteenth, OpenAI filed a supplement to a case-management statement. It said its own investigation had uncovered a possible problem involving a different worker — someone who had come to OpenAI from Apple, stayed roughly six months, and then gone back to Apple.

**A07-P02**

OpenAI's supplement gave unusually specific descriptions: engineering and manufacturing data, supplier information, detailed photographs of unreleased hardware, standards for cosmetic finishes, and reports about coatings and seals. Those descriptions came from one party's filing; they were not a public release of the underlying confidential files.

**A07-P03 · SHORT SH07 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

OpenAI's October fourteenth filing alleged that a former employee sent more than one hundred confidential files about an unreleased device to personal accounts before returning to Apple. It did not establish that those files reached Apple's corporate systems. A transfer to someone's personal account is a serious allegation. Proof of Apple using the information would be a different question.

**A07-P04**

The chronology is easy to get wrong. OpenAI described some transfers happening around June second, just after midnight in Singapore on what it identified as the employee's last workday. That was before Apple filed its lawsuit in July. October fourteenth is the date OpenAI disclosed its allegation in this court case, not the date it said every file moved.

**A07-P05**

OpenAI also said it understood that the returning employee had presented product plans to Apple executives, including operations leadership in Asia. The filing did not establish the contents of any such presentation through a judicial finding. It also did not show an Apple product using protected OpenAI information.

**A07-P06**

OpenAI pointed toward Apple's push into AI-enabled home devices as a reason for its concern. Timing, though, isn't a data trail. A new Apple product, even one announced close to the filing, does not prove that another company's secrets were used to create it.

**A07-P07**

The twist had another limit that headlines could easily bury. OpenAI did not file a formal counterclaim in that October fourteenth supplement. It asked for room to investigate and potentially bring one later. So the story was no longer a single set of accusations — but it was not yet a pair of fully litigated mirror-image lawsuits either.

**A07-P08**

Now the question was no longer only what a former Apple worker might have taken to OpenAI. It was also whether a completely different employee had taken OpenAI information out in the opposite direction. And whether that second allegation could survive the same scrutiny that OpenAI demanded for Apple's case.

### A08 — APPLE'S RESPONSE — AND THE MISSING PROOF

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S018 S016 S019*

**A08-P01**

Apple did more than issue a public denial. In a written federal court response filed October fourteenth, Apple said OpenAI had notified it on October ninth, at two in the afternoon, about the returning employee. Apple said it opened an investigation that same day. On October twelfth, Apple told OpenAI it had been unable to locate confidential OpenAI information and requested more details to run alternative searches.

**A08-P02**

Apple argued that OpenAI had not supplied those details before instead filing its supplement in court. Apple also said the new allegation was unrelated to the original lawsuit, which concerns alleged misuse of Apple's information. That is Apple's position in ECF one seventy-seven; it is not an independent court finding that no files ever left OpenAI or reached an Apple employee.

**A08-P03**

Separately, Bloomberg Law reported that Judge Davila said he was highly likely to favor an injunction for Apple. At the October fourteenth hearing, OpenAI voluntarily agreed not to use the Apple information at issue and to collect forensic images from fourteen employees' devices. The pledge did not mean OpenAI admitted it possessed or misused Apple trade secrets.

**A08-P04**

A published analysis of the court docket says the hearing minutes recorded an agreement to submit a proposed order intended to make the injunction motion unnecessary. Three stipulation proposals appeared on October fifteenth. But I have not verified the text of a signed order, so those proposals cannot be called a final issued injunction or a settlement resolving the lawsuit.

**A08-P05**

The genuine consequence was narrower and still important: Apple obtained a signed discovery order, and OpenAI reportedly accepted protective commitments while its own counter-allegation remained unresolved. Neither development turned an accusation into a verdict.

### A09 — WHAT IT COULD COST

*NON-SPOKEN SOURCES: S001 S005 S006 S016 S018 S019*

**A09-P01**

Something concrete had changed. A court ordered limited early evidence production for Apple. Bloomberg reported that OpenAI voluntarily agreed not to use disputed Apple secrets and to gather forensic copies of fourteen employees' devices. But none of the court documents reviewed established a final theft verdict, a monetary damages award, or a finding that either rival had built a product from protected material.

**A09-P02 · SHORT SH08 · ONE PARAGRAPH**

Apple won limited early evidence access in its lawsuit against OpenAI. Bloomberg also reported a voluntary OpenAI pledge not to use disputed Apple secrets and to collect forensic images of fourteen employees' devices. Those were serious procedural developments — not proof of theft. OpenAI's separate allegation involving more than one hundred files was still disputed.

**A09-P03**

What would actually change this story? Evidence identifying which files were protected; records showing who accessed, copied or disclosed them; evidence of whether any competitor received or used them; and a court decision addressing liability. The actual record did not yet supply that completed chain.

**A09-P04**

Expertise travels when an engineer changes jobs. An employer's confidential files are a different matter. But even proof that a person sent files to a private account doesn't automatically prove their next employer got them. The evidence would have to connect those steps.

**A09-P05**

That is why this dispute was bigger than a single departed employee. A partnership built around software sat beside a contest to develop new hardware. The danger was not only the headline about stolen secrets. It was that each company now had to show exactly where knowledge ended and protected information began.

**A09-P06**

Apple started the lawsuit. OpenAI then made an allegation pointing toward Apple. And the court compelled limited evidence disclosure while the parties discussed protective promises. The direction of the accusation changed. The legal result did not flip. What it ultimately costs depends on what the remaining evidence proves.
