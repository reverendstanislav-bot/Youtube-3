### A00 — THE REVERSAL

*NON-SPOKEN: sources S001,S005,S006,S018,S023,S024; claim IDs C001,C026,C033,C035,C041,C049,C060,C061.*

**A00-P01 · SHORT SH01 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple sued OpenAI over alleged stolen hardware secrets. Then OpenAI alleged a worker returning to Apple had sent more than a hundred confidential OpenAI files to personal accounts. Did that prove Apple had the files? No. OpenAI's filing did not establish that. Two rival allegations pointed in opposite directions — but the judge would end up deciding something very different.

**A00-P02**

The two companies had announced a partnership that brought ChatGPT to Siri. Yet their hardware ambitions competed. This fight was not about who invented the better chatbot. It was about what confidential hardware information, if any, followed engineers to a rival.

**A00-P03**

Apple claimed it had a digital trail: a departed engineer who still could reach internal systems, and dozens of hardware files. That sounds straightforward. Until you hear how OpenAI explained the same access.

**A00-P04**

First, how did software partners end up fighting over physical devices? And what exactly had Apple accused its former employees of doing?

### A01 — FROM PARTNERS TO HARDWARE RIVALS

*NON-SPOKEN: official 2024 announcement S017, complaint S001 and OpenAI filing S006; claim IDs C010,C011,C036,C037,C051.*

**A01-P01**

In June twenty twenty-four, Apple and OpenAI announced that ChatGPT would integrate with Siri and other Apple software. The partnership did not merge their engineering teams or their separate interests in consumer hardware. That is how a software partner could become a courtroom opponent.

**A01-P02**

Making an AI device involves more than a model. Engineers learn which finishes last, which battery designs fail, and which suppliers can manufacture a part. That knowledge can be valuable to a competitor. But professional experience is not the same as an employer's confidential records. The difference matters commercially. Professional expertise can help build a new device, while private specifications or supplier records may require separate protection. That is the boundary the dispute tests.

**A01-P03**

An engineer may carry skills into a new job. A confidential drawing, manufacturing plan or protected supplier file is different. Hiring from another company is not, by itself, theft.

**A01-P04**

Apple said this distinction mattered because it had more than a theory about where people went. Its complaint described post-departure system access and recruiting requests involving confidential hardware. The case therefore did not depend on the fact of hiring alone. It depended on identifying protected material and connecting that material to a specific act of access, transfer or misuse.

**A01-P05**

That answered why the partners became rivals. The new question was whether Apple had enough specifics to bring a federal lawsuit.

### A02 — THE LAWSUIT APPLE FILED

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S005; claim IDs C001,C002,C003,C008,C012,C013,C048.*

**A02-P01**

On July tenth, twenty twenty-six, Apple filed a civil lawsuit in San Jose. It named former Apple employees Chang Liu and Tang Yew Tan, along with OpenAI entities and io Products, a business connected to OpenAI's hardware effort.

**A02-P02**

Apple wanted to prevent alleged misuse before trial. It sought a preliminary injunction and, separately, early access to evidence that might support its concerns. An injunction can address feared urgent harm before trial. Early discovery instead concerns when documents and testimony may be obtained. Those requests have separate procedural standards.

**A02-P03**

The complaint contained six civil claims: four under federal trade-secret law, and two involving former employees' intellectual-property agreements. These were allegations, not verdicts. The case turned on the conduct beneath the legal counts.

**A02-P04**

Apple described two trails. One was digital access after an engineer left. The other involved recruiting conversations, including an alleged request for physical Apple parts. The allegations connected employee departures to engineering information and interview activity. But the connection described in a complaint must still be tested against records and the defendants' responses.

**A02-P05**

Apple also requested financial relief. But a request for damages did not prove a particular sum lost, and no reviewed court order awarded one. That distinction matters for the cost at the center of the story: a requested remedy is not money lost, money paid, or a final result.

**A02-P06**

The first question was remarkably concrete: if Chang Liu had already left Apple, how was he allegedly still getting into its systems?

### A03 — THE DOZENS OF APPLE FILES

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001; claim IDs C003,C004,C005,C006,C007,C048.*

**A03-P01**

Apple alleged a rare authentication problem let Liu access confidential hardware information after departure. The complaint also described access using another Apple computer. Those were Apple's accusations about the route, not court findings. A technical ability to log in would not by itself establish the former employee's permission to use that access, much less actual use of a protected design by his next employer.

**A03-P02**

Apple said Liu downloaded dozens of Apple-origin hardware files. Those files were not the more than one hundred OpenAI-origin files that would later appear in a separate allegation.

**A03-P03**

Apple further alleged he retained a company laptop and advised a job candidate about avoiding attention when leaving. In Apple's account, the details suggested more than one isolated access event. The laptop and other machine were separate details in Apple's version of events. Each could matter to the timeline, but neither alone proved what happened to a particular confidential file.

**A03-P04**

But the existence of access does not answer everything. Was it permitted? Did particular files move to the next employer? Did anyone use protected information in a new product? Each link would need evidence. A login can establish an account was used; a download can identify a file. The contested step is tracing a protected design into a rival's possession or product work.

**A03-P05**

So Apple had a story worth testing, not a verdict built from a download count. OpenAI's response supplied an alternative explanation for the same activity.

**A03-P06**

What if the employee could still log in because Apple had not shut off access properly — and colleagues were still asking for help?

### A04 — OPENAI'S DEFENSE AND THE FORENSIC FIGHT

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S002,S003,S004,S007; claim IDs C015,C016,C017,C019,C020,C021,C022,C023,C024.*

**A04-P01**

OpenAI argued that Apple's offboarding had sometimes left access active. It also said Apple colleagues asked Liu for help after he left. If those assertions were accurate, some activity Apple found suspicious could have a different context. That directly challenged Apple's interpretation of its logs. Requests for help from former colleagues could affect how the same activity was understood without automatically making every later access lawful.

**A04-P02**

OpenAI published selected redacted messages to support its version. Those excerpts were a party's evidence, not a judicial ruling that every access was authorized. Redacted excerpts may support a defense theory, but they do not necessarily show every conversation, attachment or step in the technical chronology. That is why the fuller record mattered.

**A04-P03**

Now there were two interpretations of the digital trail: Apple's account of unauthorized access and OpenAI's account of lingering permissions and requests from Apple colleagues. The surrounding records mattered.

**A04-P04**

The forensic disagreement also involved files, AI tools and suspected deletion. The court's October thirteenth discovery order did not resolve whether evidence had been destroyed. The parties differed not only about what the machines recorded, but also about the meaning of those traces. The judge left that disagreement open in the discovery ruling.

**A04-P05**

OpenAI and the other defendants moved to dismiss the suit and fought Apple's request for urgent relief. These were litigation positions, not outcomes.

**A04-P06**

Then came an allegation that needed no technical log to sound serious: during recruiting, Apple said someone asked for actual physical hardware.

### A05 — THE INTERVIEWS AND THE PHYSICAL PARTS

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S002,S007; claim IDs C007,C008,C009,C010,C011,C018.*

**A05-P01**

The recruiting part of the complaint centered on Tang Yew Tan, a former Apple executive who had spent more than two decades there. Apple alleged OpenAI's hardware recruiting went beyond ordinary questions about professional experience. Under Apple's theory, the interview process mattered because it could reveal what OpenAI wanted to know about ongoing hardware projects, rather than only testing general engineering talent.

**A05-P02**

Apple described a candidate's surprise at a request involving actual Apple hardware. But the allegation concerned what was asked during an interview, not evidence that a part was delivered.

**A05-P03 · SHORT SH02 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Two words in Apple's lawsuit stand out: 'Actual parts.' Apple alleged that former Apple executive Tang Yew Tan sought physical Apple hardware during recruiting. Did that prove someone delivered an Apple component? No. The complaint described a request, not a confirmed handoff. But it gave Apple a specific reason to demand the interview documents — and ask what candidates were really told to bring.

**A05-P04**

OpenAI disputed wrongdoing. Asking an engineer to explain a design is different from asking them to bring protected hardware from a former employer. The actual interview materials could help distinguish what happened. Written guides or candidate materials could distinguish those explanations more reliably than either company's characterization. That is why the real interview documents became a concrete target for discovery.

**A05-P05**

That is why Apple requested early production of interview guides, presentations and related records. The documents might provide the context that an allegation alone could not.

**A05-P06**

Would the judge grant early access to those records? This was the first question a court order could actually answer.

### A06 — WHAT THE JUDGE REALLY ORDERED

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S005; claim IDs C024,C025,C026,C027,C028,C029,C030,C031,C032.*

**A06-P01**

On October thirteenth, Judge Edward Davila partially granted Apple's request for expedited discovery. That meant specified evidence could be produced earlier than the ordinary schedule.

**A06-P02**

For certain material already collected, the order set a five-day deadline. Other permitted responses had a fourteen-day window, including specified forensic, interview and LINE-message material. These deadlines did not mean the files had already been produced or that their contents proved wrongdoing. They only defined an earlier schedule for specific evidence.

**A06-P03**

The judge also permitted limited depositions. But Apple did not receive everything it wanted: sweeping early requests about battery, power management and metal-finishing research were rejected, while other categories needed narrower definitions. The permitted questioning covered named witnesses and corporate testimony on defined topics. Requests depending on the broad term Apple Originated Information were deferred until narrowed, not granted without limits.

**A06-P04**

The order did not decide whether evidence had been destroyed or whether Apple proved its theft allegations. It was about access to records, not the final result of the case.

**A06-P05**

So the answer was specific: Apple could get certain evidence sooner, but not unrestricted access to OpenAI's research. The separate request for a preliminary injunction still had its own path.

**A06-P06**

Before that evidence could settle Apple's concerns, however, OpenAI reported a different possible file transfer — involving its own confidential material.

**A06-P07**

And the employee in that new allegation was headed the other way: back to Apple.

### A07 — THE HUNDRED FILES, IN THE OTHER DIRECTION

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S006; claim IDs C033,C034,C035,C036,C037,C038,C039,C040,C041,C042,C047,C048.*

**A07-P01**

On October fourteenth, OpenAI filed a supplement to a case-management statement. It described an unnamed employee who had moved from Apple to OpenAI, stayed for roughly six months, and then returned to Apple. This was a different employee from the people named in Apple's complaint. Mixing the workers or combining the two companies' file counts would turn separate allegations into a false single leak.

**A07-P02**

According to OpenAI, the files concerned an unreleased device, engineering images, manufacturing details, suppliers, finishes and coatings. Those descriptions were allegations in its filing, not public access to the confidential files themselves. Those categories made OpenAI's concern concrete, but the underlying files were not released as public proof that an Apple corporate system ever received them.

**A07-P03 · SHORT SH03 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Then OpenAI raised its own file allegation. It said an employee returning to Apple sent more than one hundred confidential OpenAI files about unreleased hardware to personal accounts. That sounded like the Apple lawsuit in reverse. But the filing did not establish that Apple received the files or that Apple engineers used them. The alleged personal transfer and a company's use are different claims.

**A07-P04**

OpenAI said some transfers occurred around June second in Singapore, on the employee's last workday. That was before Apple's July lawsuit. October fourteenth was the date OpenAI disclosed the allegation in this case, not the alleged date of every transfer. The distinction matters because the alleged transfers came before the public fight. The October filing revealed OpenAI's accusation in court; it did not move the alleged transfer date forward.

**A07-P05**

OpenAI also said it understood that the employee had presented its product plans to Apple leadership. That was OpenAI's understanding, not a court finding of what Apple executives actually received.

**A07-P06**

OpenAI pointed to Apple's AI home-device activity as a source of concern. But an announcement of a device is not evidence that another company's confidential design was used.

**A07-P07**

The filing was not a countersuit. OpenAI requested time to investigate possible counterclaims; its October fourteenth supplement did not formally bring one.

**A07-P08**

Had those files reached Apple? Apple said it had already started looking — days before OpenAI filed the allegation in court.

### A08 — APPLE'S RESPONSE — AND THE MISSING PROOF

*NON-SPOKEN: S018 original Apple ECF177 response; S016 Bloomberg reported Oct14 hearing; S023/S024 official federal district-court docket ECF188/189 entered Oct16; claims C043–C046,C053–C061. Full Oct16 order texts not separately read.*

**A08-P01**

In Apple's October fourteenth response, it said OpenAI first notified it on Friday, October ninth, at two in the afternoon. Apple said its investigation began that day.

**A08-P02**

Apple said it told OpenAI on Monday, October twelfth, that its searches had not found confidential OpenAI material. It requested more details for additional searches, and said OpenAI had not replied before its filing. Apple's response was its own account of its investigation, not a judicial certification of the scope or completeness of a search of all possible personal destinations.

**A08-P03**

That answered what Apple said its own search found. It did not prove the alleged personal-account transfers were false, nor did OpenAI's allegation establish use of those files inside Apple.

**A08-P04**

At the October fourteenth hearing, Bloomberg Law reported Judge Davila appeared likely to grant Apple a preliminary injunction. It also reported OpenAI promised not to use disputed Apple material and would gather forensic images from fourteen employees' devices. But that was a hearing report, not the final signed order.

**A08-P05**

Two days later came the actual court result. On October sixteenth, the judge approved a joint stipulation about document preservation and forensic inspection and found Apple's preliminary-injunction motion moot. A second signed order approved expedited discovery. The urgent request no longer required a merits ruling — but neither side's theft allegations had been decided.

### A09 — WHAT IT COULD COST

*NON-SPOKEN: sources S001,S005,S006,S018,S023,S024; claim IDs C012,C013,C014,C041,C042,C049,C051,C060,C061.* Mootness was not a judgment that Apple's complaint was false, and it was not a finding of theft. It described what happened to this specific urgent motion after the evidence-related stipulation was approved.

**A09-P01**

What did that outcome actually cost? The verified consequence was a legal process: preserving evidence, forensic inspection and earlier discovery. The court did not assign a public dollar value to those burdens.

**A09-P02**

The judge did not rule that OpenAI had stolen Apple's secrets, or that Apple used OpenAI's. Instead, he approved agreements governing evidence while Apple's urgent injunction request became moot.

**A09-P03 · SHORT SH04 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple asked a federal judge for a preliminary injunction against OpenAI. But on October sixteenth, the judge approved a joint agreement on preserving evidence and forensic inspection, and found that injunction request moot. Another order approved expedited discovery. Was that a ruling that OpenAI stole Apple's secrets? No. The judge established how evidence would be handled without deciding the theft allegations.

**A09-P04**

Apple had asked for financial relief, but the reviewed filings and procedural orders did not establish any damages award. The court's confirmed actions imposed evidence obligations, not a verified price tag. That does not mean the litigation lacked potential financial stakes. Any later award would need a legal basis and proof of protected information, misconduct and harm — none established by these procedural orders.

**A09-P05**

A downloaded file does not automatically prove a rival used it. A presentation allegation does not establish what a manager saw. And a motion becoming moot does not decide whether an alleged secret was misused.

**A09-P06**

What remains is the evidence itself: the preserved devices, interview records and any documents connecting a protected file to a new employer's activity. The court's orders set up that examination; they did not replace it.

**A09-P07**

Apple and OpenAI could partner on software and compete over hardware. Apple's first alleged trail was tested against OpenAI's offboarding defense. OpenAI's second allegation ran toward Apple, which described an earlier unsuccessful search. Finally, a judge ordered preservation and discovery while Apple's injunction motion became moot. The case did not reverse into a theft verdict. It became a fight over provable evidence.
