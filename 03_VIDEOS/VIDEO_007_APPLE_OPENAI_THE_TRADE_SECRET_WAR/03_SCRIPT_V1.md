### A00 — THE REVERSAL

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S005,S006; claim IDs C001,C026,C033,C035,C041,C049.*

**A00-P01 · SHORT SH01 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple accused OpenAI of taking its hardware secrets. Three months later, OpenAI told a federal court that more than one hundred of its own confidential files had been sent to a former employee's personal accounts. The employee had returned to Apple. But the filing did not prove that Apple received or used those files.

**A00-P02**

This wasn't a judgment. It wasn't even a countersuit from OpenAI. It was a second allegation, arriving in the middle of a case that Apple had started.

**A00-P03**

And the timing made it explosive. Just one day earlier, a judge had ordered the OpenAI side to hand over certain records. Not everything Apple wanted. Just enough to begin testing some serious claims.

**A00-P04**

So how did two companies that once worked together end up trading allegations about secret hardware designs? And, more importantly, what could the documents actually prove?

### A01 — FROM PARTNERS TO HARDWARE RIVALS

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S006,S009; claim IDs C010,C011,C036,C037,C051.*

**A01-P01**

Apple and OpenAI have something unusual in common: both have enormous reasons to care about the next device people put in their homes, pockets, or workplaces. Apple already makes consumer hardware at extraordinary scale. OpenAI has been building a team to turn artificial intelligence into physical products.

**A01-P02**

These companies also have a history of cooperation. But cooperation on AI services doesn't mean they have the same interests when the conversation turns to hardware. A company can be a partner in one product and a competitor in the next. And once both want talented engineers who have spent years solving very specific manufacturing problems, the lines get difficult.

**A01-P03**

Imagine an engineer who knows which coating survives a thousand tests, which supplier can manufacture a difficult part, or why one battery design failed. That person can take their experience to a new job. The question is whether they can also take private files, drawings, and instructions belonging to the old employer.

**A01-P04**

Those are very different things. It is not illegal just to hire people who once worked for a competitor. But a trade-secret dispute can begin when one side alleges that particular protected information came along with them.

**A01-P05**

And here, Apple didn't simply accuse a rival of hiring its engineers. It said the line between a person's expertise and Apple's confidential material had been crossed. To understand that accusation, we need to meet the people named in its complaint.

### A02 — THE LAWSUIT APPLE FILED

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S005; claim IDs C001,C002,C003,C008,C012,C013,C048.*

**A02-P01**

On July tenth, twenty twenty-six, Apple filed a civil lawsuit in federal court in San Jose. The defendants included two former Apple employees, Chang Liu and Tang Yew Tan, together with OpenAI entities and io Products, a business connected to OpenAI's consumer hardware effort.

**A02-P02**

The filing laid out six civil claims. Four were brought under the federal Defend Trade Secrets Act. Two concerned the former employees' intellectual-property agreements. It was not a criminal indictment, and filing those claims did not mean the allegations had been proved.

**A02-P03 · SHORT SH02 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple filed six civil claims against former employees and the OpenAI side in July twenty twenty-six. Four were trade-secret claims. Two concerned employee agreements. Those numbers sound decisive, but a complaint is where a company states what it wants to prove. It is not six convictions, and it is not six findings by a judge.

**A02-P04**

Apple's theory focused on more than a single folder. In its telling, recruiting interviews, access to old systems, and specific knowledge about future devices all pointed toward a larger pattern. Apple wanted the court to stop alleged misuse and order the return or protection of information it considered confidential.

**A02-P05**

It also sought financial remedies. But the complaint did not establish a specific dollar amount that OpenAI had already cost Apple. A demand for damages is not the same thing as a damages award.

**A02-P06**

One allegation, though, had a detail so specific that it would become central to the evidence fight. Apple said an engineer had been able to reach internal material after leaving. And it claimed that unusual access had turned into downloaded files.

### A03 — THE DOZENS OF APPLE FILES

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001; claim IDs C003,C004,C005,C006,C007,C048.*

**A03-P01**

Chang Liu had worked on Apple hardware. After his departure, Apple alleges, a flaw in the way one part of its authentication system handled access left a route into confidential areas. Apple says Liu used that opening to access information and download dozens of files related to its hardware work.

**A03-P02 · SHORT SH03 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple's complaint describes a rare access problem after engineer Chang Liu left the company. Apple alleges that he used it to reach private hardware material and download dozens of files. Dozens — not the one hundred-plus OpenAI would describe later. And at this point, these were Apple's allegations, not a judge's findings.

**A03-P03**

That difference in the numbers matters. The first group is Apple-origin material that Apple says Liu accessed. The second group, which we will reach later, concerns different OpenAI-origin documents and an unnamed employee moving in the opposite direction. They are not one giant collection of stolen files.

**A03-P04**

Apple also alleges that Liu kept an Apple laptop after departure and used another machine to reach internal resources. And it says he gave advice to an employee considering OpenAI about avoiding attention when leaving Apple. These are allegations that need to be tested against what the systems recorded, the surrounding messages, and the defendants' responses.

**A03-P05**

There's a second question hiding inside the first. Even if a file was downloaded, where did it go? Was it opened at the new employer? Did it contain a legally protected secret? Was it used to design a competing product? Those are separate steps. A dramatic screenshot of a download count cannot answer all of them.

**A03-P06**

Apple believed the pattern was serious enough to sue. OpenAI said the story behind the records was not what Apple was claiming.

### A04 — OPENAI'S DEFENSE AND THE FORENSIC FIGHT

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S002,S003,S004,S007; claim IDs C015,C016,C017,C019,C020,C021,C022,C023,C024.*

**A04-P01**

OpenAI's response wasn't simply that it wanted to compete with Apple. It challenged Apple's interpretation of the supposed evidence. OpenAI argued that access for departing employees could remain active because of Apple's own offboarding processes. That raises a basic but consequential question: if a system still lets someone in, does that explain access, or does the context show it was unauthorized?

**A04-P02 · SHORT SH04 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

OpenAI offers a very different account of Chang Liu's access to Apple systems. It says access sometimes remained active after employees left and that Apple colleagues even asked Liu for help. That is OpenAI's defense, not a court finding. The difference matters because access, permission, and misuse are three separate questions.

**A04-P03**

On its website, OpenAI also published selected messages that it said showed Apple colleagues seeking assistance from Liu after his departure. Those excerpts serve as OpenAI's account. Without the full underlying record and the court's assessment, they cannot independently settle the dispute.

**A04-P04**

The battle became more technical. The parties disagreed over what forensic material showed about the use of files, AI tools, and possible deletion of information. The defense rejected Apple's interpretation of key findings and questioned whether evidence showed that a contested file was used in the way Apple suggested.

**A04-P05**

In August, the defendants moved to dismiss the case. They also opposed Apple's request for an early injunction. But a motion to dismiss is just a request. An opposition brief is just one side's argument. Neither filing by itself ends a lawsuit.

**A04-P06**

What makes this interesting is the unusual position both sides now occupied. Apple was insisting a collection of digital traces revealed misuse. OpenAI was saying Apple had misunderstood what its own systems and people had done. The judge would have to decide what information the parties should exchange before those competing stories could be properly tested.

### A05 — THE INTERVIEWS AND THE PHYSICAL PARTS

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S002,S007; claim IDs C007,C008,C009,C010,C011,C018.*

**A05-P01**

There was another former Apple employee at the center of the case: Tang Yew Tan, a veteran of more than two decades at Apple. Apple alleged that sensitive knowledge about its hardware programs came up around OpenAI's efforts to hire people from the company.

**A05-P02**

Some of the most striking accusations concerned technical interviews. Apple alleged that information about confidential projects had been solicited and that requests were made involving actual hardware parts. But there is a crucial distance between saying someone asked for a part and proving that any part was delivered, examined, or built into a competing device.

**A05-P03 · SHORT SH05 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple alleges that confidential hardware information and even physical parts were discussed during OpenAI recruiting. The employee at the center of that allegation was former Apple executive Tang Yew Tan. But asking about a part is not proof that anyone delivered it. And Apple still had to establish which information was protected and how it was used.

**A05-P04**

OpenAI disputes wrongdoing. Its position is that experienced people can bring their professional skills with them, while confidential information from another employer is off limits. Apple argues the details in its complaint show conduct that went beyond an ordinary interview or ordinary job change.

**A05-P05**

That is why the actual interview records became so important. If the sessions were ordinary assessments of professional experience, the notes would support one picture. If the documents recorded requests for internal product information, Apple would argue they supported another. The contents and circumstances mattered far more than a dramatic accusation in a headline.

**A05-P06**

And now the case reached a point where the judge would decide how much of those records Apple could see early.

### A06 — WHAT THE JUDGE REALLY ORDERED

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S005; claim IDs C024,C025,C026,C027,C028,C029,C030,C031,C032.*

**A06-P01**

On October thirteenth, Judge Edward Davila issued a ten-page order. Apple had asked for expedited discovery — which means access to certain records earlier than the ordinary timetable. It wanted evidence it believed could help evaluate its separate request for a preliminary injunction.

**A06-P02**

The court gave Apple part of what it sought. It ordered production of specified forensic images already collected for the litigation, along with materials used to guide interviews of candidates coming from Apple. It also permitted limited depositions, including testimony from the two former Apple employees and other identified witnesses.

**A06-P03 · SHORT SH06 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

On October thirteenth, a federal judge gave Apple only part of its request for early evidence from OpenAI. Some forensic records, interview materials, and limited depositions were allowed. Broad demands for research-and-development documents were not accelerated, and other requests were deferred. This was a discovery ruling — not a verdict that OpenAI stole Apple's secrets.

**A06-P04**

Some of Apple's more sweeping early demands about research into metal finishes, batteries, and power management did not receive the accelerated treatment Apple wanted. Other requests depended on a phrase the judge found too vague: Apple-originated information. The court required a more tailored definition before deciding how far those requests could reach.

**A06-P05**

The judge also declined, in that discovery decision, to resolve the opposing forensic experts' arguments about whether evidence had been destroyed. The order did not decide the merits of Apple's theft allegations, and it was not a ruling granting Apple's separate preliminary injunction request.

**A06-P06**

Still, it changed the practical situation. The companies were moving beyond public statements and into narrower categories of records that had to be produced. Questions about who interviewed whom, what guides were used, and what had been collected would be tested against documents, not just press releases.

**A06-P07**

Then, before the next day was over, the direction of the story changed.

### A07 — THE HUNDRED FILES, IN THE OTHER DIRECTION

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S006; claim IDs C033,C034,C035,C036,C037,C038,C039,C040,C041,C042,C047,C048.*

**A07-P01**

On October fourteenth, OpenAI filed a supplement to a case-management statement. It said its own investigation had uncovered a possible problem involving a different worker — someone who had come to OpenAI from Apple, stayed roughly six months, and then gone back to Apple.

**A07-P02**

According to OpenAI, that employee sent more than one hundred highly confidential files to personal accounts. OpenAI described materials relating to at least one unreleased device: engineering details, manufacturing information, suppliers, finishes, coatings, and detailed product images.

**A07-P03 · SHORT SH07 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

OpenAI's October fourteenth filing alleged that a former employee sent more than one hundred confidential files about an unreleased device to personal accounts before returning to Apple. It did not establish that those files reached Apple's corporate systems. A transfer to someone's personal account is a serious allegation. Proof of Apple using the information would be a different question.

**A07-P04**

The chronology is easy to get wrong. OpenAI described some transfers happening around June second, just after midnight in Singapore on what it identified as the employee's last workday. That was before Apple filed its lawsuit in July. October fourteenth is the date OpenAI disclosed its allegation in this court case, not the date it said every file moved.

**A07-P05**

OpenAI also said it understood that the former employee had made a presentation concerning its product plans to Apple leadership. But that was OpenAI's stated understanding, not something the court confirmed. And the filing did not establish that Apple engineers possessed or used OpenAI's confidential material to design any product.

**A07-P06**

OpenAI pointed toward Apple's push into AI-enabled home devices as a reason for its concern. Timing, though, isn't a data trail. A new Apple product, even one announced close to the filing, does not prove that another company's secrets were used to create it.

**A07-P07**

The twist had another limit that headlines could easily bury. OpenAI did not file a formal counterclaim in that October fourteenth supplement. It asked for room to investigate and potentially bring one later. So the story was no longer a single set of accusations — but it was not yet a pair of fully litigated mirror-image lawsuits either.

**A07-P08**

Now the question was no longer only what a former Apple worker might have taken to OpenAI. It was also whether a completely different employee had taken OpenAI information out in the opposite direction. And whether that second allegation could survive the same scrutiny that OpenAI demanded for Apple's case.

### A08 — APPLE'S RESPONSE — AND THE MISSING PROOF

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S008,S010,S013,S014,S015; claim IDs C040,C043,C044,C045,C046,C049.*

**A08-P01**

Apple pushed back. Reuters reported that Apple said it had looked into OpenAI's allegation and found no evidence of OpenAI confidential material in its systems. Apple also maintains policies intended to keep new hires from bringing protected information from previous employers into the company.

**A08-P02**

That denial has to be treated just as carefully as OpenAI's claim. A company saying it found no evidence is reporting the result of its own review. It is not a judicial finding that the alleged transfer could not have happened. And OpenAI's concern is not a judicial finding that the material was used.

**A08-P03**

There is a final procedural catch. Reports from the October fourteenth hearing suggest that Apple and the defendants also discussed the pending injunction dispute and proposed agreements. But without the signed orders and complete updated docket in hand, we cannot present any proposed language as a binding outcome.

**A08-P04**

What we can say is narrower, and stronger. Apple's original complaint, the defendants' responses, the October thirteenth discovery order, and OpenAI's October fourteenth filing are different kinds of documents. A claim, a denial, a motion, and an order carry different weight.

**A08-P05**

To know whether either side's secrets ended up in a rival device, a viewer needs more than the loudest accusation. They need a chain of actual evidence.

### A09 — WHAT IT COULD COST

*NON-SPOKEN: primary sources S001,S005,S006,S008; claim IDs C012,C013,C014,C041,C042,C049,C051.*

**A09-P01**

The title of this story suggests an obvious scoreboard. Apple versus OpenAI. One side takes a hit. The other wins. But that's not where this case stood in the documents we could verify in October twenty twenty-six.

**A09-P02**

Apple sought orders protecting its confidential information and asked for financial relief. OpenAI disputed Apple's claims and raised its own separate concern about more than one hundred files. The judge authorized a narrower exchange of evidence. None of that establishes a dollar amount the companies had paid one another or proves that either built a product using the other's protected work.

**A09-P03 · SHORT SH08 (EXACT ONE-PARAGRAPH EXTRACT)**

Apple and OpenAI are fighting over allegations about confidential hardware information. Apple asked the court for damages and protection of its secrets. But the verified filings contained no final dollar award or settlement amount for this dispute. The cost may be significant. What it actually cost — and who is responsible — has not been decided in those records.

**A09-P04**

There are three questions that can turn the case. First, which information was genuinely confidential and legally protected? Second, what did the records show about access, authorization, and copying? Third, did protected information make it into a competitor's hands or products — and what harm, if any, followed?

**A09-P05**

The first filing points in one direction. The second allegation points in the other. The truth could be more complicated than either side's public version. And it is possible for a court to establish only part of a story: one person's actions without proving a company's use, or a failure of controls without proving a lost product advantage.

**A09-P06**

That is what makes this fight more revealing than a simple story about files. It's about what happens when two companies depend on the same kind of people, pursue the same kind of hardware, and disagree about who owns the knowledge those people carry.

**A09-P07**

For now, the twist is real. The accusations are real. The unresolved questions are real. The verdict people might imagine is not. And the most important evidence may be the evidence neither side has yet put in public view.
