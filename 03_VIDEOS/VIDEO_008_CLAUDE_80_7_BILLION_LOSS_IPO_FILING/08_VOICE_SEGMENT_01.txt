Claude's maker reported an eighty-point-seven-billion-dollar loss. But its loss from actually operating the business was six hundred ninety-three million. Same company. Same six months. That difference is the first clue that the terrifying headline is not the bill for running an AI model.

The main reason is a seventy-two-point-four-billion-dollar revaluation of financing instruments. It changed the accounting result; it wasn't seventy-two billion paid to data centers. So the headline loses its simplest explanation. Yet on the day Anthropic published those figures, something even stranger happened.

On October twenty-sixth, the day its preliminary IPO filing became public, Bank of America, Citi, Morgan Stanley and Blackstone announced a sixty-three-billion-dollar financing structure for computing equipment intended for Anthropic. A company reporting a giant loss was also preparing for an enormous hardware expansion.

That was not sixty-three billion dollars wired into Anthropic's bank account. The partners described a new vehicle that would buy Broadcom processors and networking equipment and lease it to Anthropic. Deliveries were expected from twenty twenty-seven. Financing could be organized today. Paying customers for every future machine could not.

And the filing disclosed a specific five-year Broadcom equipment lease with about one hundred twenty-five-point-two billion dollars in estimated rent, excluding interest. This is not a second pile of cash to add to the financing announcement. It is a different measure of a future commitment. The mystery now has consequences: what if the customers never arrive in the numbers anticipated?

Why sign up for capacity this large? Look at the revenue. In the first half of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic reported one-point-one-six-three billion dollars. In the first half of twenty twenty-six, it reported sixteen-point-two-eight-six billion. Approximately fourteen times as much in the comparable period. That is actual reported sales, not an investor valuation.

But higher sales don't remove the physical limit. Claude answers through processors, networks and data centers that have to exist before the request is sent. Anthropic reported eight-point-nine-four-five billion dollars in computing and infrastructure costs for that half-year. More customers bring revenue and an immediate demand for more capacity.

Four major financiers announced sixty-three billion dollars for AI equipment intended for Anthropic. But the plan wasn't to transfer that cash to Claude's maker. A special vehicle would purchase Broadcom hardware and lease it to Anthropic. The money finances machines. It does not guarantee customers.

Imagine the decision facing whoever forecasts demand. Reserve too little computing power and a growing customer may be unable to use the service. Reserve too much and equipment can remain underused while contractual payments continue. In a market changing this fast, the accurate forecast matters as much as the technology.

And that is why the revenue growth is not a happy ending. It explains why Anthropic might take the risk in the first place. The filing tells us how much demand has already grown; it cannot promise what customers will buy in twenty twenty-eight. For that, we have to look at the actual lease terms.
