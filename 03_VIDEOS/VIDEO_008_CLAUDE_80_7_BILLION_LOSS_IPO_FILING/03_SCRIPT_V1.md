## A00 — THE NUMBER THAT SHOULDN'T MAKE SENSE

<!-- P001 | A00-B01 | CLM001 | SRC002 -->
On October twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-six, Anthropic filed papers for a potential stock market debut. The company behind Claude was growing fast. But the first number in its financial story was staggering. More than eighty billion dollars in reported losses. How does a business get there?

<!-- P002 | A00-B02 | CLM004 | SRC001 -->
The exact number: eighty billion, seven hundred forty-eight million dollars. That's Anthropic's reported net loss for the first six months of twenty twenty-six. And it seems to tell a story of extraordinary expense. Until you look at the next line.

<!-- P003 | A00-B03 | CLM005 | SRC001 -->
For those same six months, Anthropic's operating loss was just six hundred ninety-three million dollars. Still a loss, but not remotely eighty billion. Same company, same reporting period, very different financial measures.

<!-- P004 | A00-B04 | CLM006 | SRC001 -->
A single charge for changes in the value of financing instruments was seventy-two billion, three hundred eighty-eight million dollars. That charge had almost nothing to do with the price of answering one more question in Claude.

<!-- P005 | A00-B05 | CLM004|CLM005|CLM006 | SRC001 -->
So the real question isn't whether Anthropic physically spent eighty billion dollars operating Claude. Its filing doesn't say that. It's what the financing structure reveals about an AI business buying enormous amounts of future computing power—and who controls that business when it asks the public to invest.

## A01 — HOW CLAUDE MAKES MONEY

<!-- P006 | A01-B01 | CLM003|CLM032 | SRC001 -->
To understand why those financing agreements matter, start with the product. Claude looks like a conversation on a screen, but Anthropic also sells access to its models for companies building software and developers connecting AI to real workflows. Each use requires computation somewhere. Customer demand can produce revenue, but making the model available requires computers that are powerful, expensive, and often difficult to obtain quickly.

<!-- P007 | A01-B02 | CLM003 | SRC001 -->
In the first half of twenty twenty-five, Anthropic reported one billion, one hundred sixty-three million dollars of revenue. Already a substantial amount for a business founded only a few years earlier. The important point is that we are using exactly six months here. Comparing like periods lets us see the pace of expansion without inflating it with a forecast or a carefully selected month.

<!-- P008 | A01-B03 | CLM003 | SRC001 -->
In the first half of twenty twenty-six, revenue reached sixteen billion, two hundred eighty-six million dollars. About fourteen times the comparable figure a year earlier. This is reported revenue from the business, not a fundraising announcement. It's why an IPO could attract enormous interest. A product capable of producing this much additional revenue in twelve months forces both customers and infrastructure suppliers to make decisions much faster.

<!-- P009 | A01-B04 | CLM018 | SRC001 -->
But there is a physical bill attached. The filing reports eight billion, nine hundred forty-five million dollars in computing and infrastructure cost for that same half-year. Running an advanced AI model involves processors, networking equipment, data-center facilities, and continuous engineering. Not every machine must belong to Anthropic. The company can obtain computing power from other businesses. Either way, capacity isn't an abstract number on a balance sheet.

<!-- P010 | A01-B05 | CLM021|CLM032 | SRC001 -->
A customer might increase its Claude usage this month, then reduce it in the next. A data-center project doesn't necessarily follow that rhythm. Specialized equipment must be ordered; installations take time; long-term capacity needs to be reserved. When demand grows this fast, a company faces an uncomfortable choice: reserve computing power before you know exactly what customers will want, or risk being unable to serve them when they arrive.

## A02 — THE ACCOUNTING REVERSAL

<!-- P011 | A02-B01 | CLM003|CLM005 | SRC001 -->
Go back to the operating numbers. Take sixteen-point-two-eight-six billion dollars of revenue and subtract the sixteen-point-nine-seven-nine billion dollars in reported operating expenses. That gives the six-hundred-ninety-three-million-dollar operating loss. This is an accounting measure of the services and operations in the period. It isn't a statement about whether every investor-funded dollar was spent or whether every long-term lease payment has already come due.

<!-- P012 | A02-B02 | CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Now imagine financing that includes a right to convert into shares. Its value to a holder may change as expectations about the business change. Accounting rules can require related obligations to be revalued. Anthropic reported a seventy-two-point-three-eight-eight-billion-dollar negative change involving convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and agreements for future equity. That figure sits below operating income. It is not seventy-two billion dollars of invoices for electricity or model training.

<!-- P013 | A02-B03 | CLM007|CLM009 | SRC001 -->
The remaining lines matter as well. Anthropic reported one-point-one-one-one billion dollars of net interest income, another eight-point-seven-five-seven billion dollars of net expense, and twenty-one million dollars of income-tax expense. Together with the operating loss and that massive valuation charge, they reconcile to eighty billion, seven hundred forty-eight million dollars in net losses. No missing magic number is needed to explain the arithmetic.

<!-- P014 | A02-B04 | CLM004|CLM005 | SRC001 -->
There are two misleading interpretations to avoid. First, you cannot simply declare that eighty billion dollars in cash vanished while Claude was running. Second, you cannot declare Anthropic profitable merely because the largest expense is an accounting revaluation. The first-half operating figure was still negative. A potential investor should understand both the costs of the operating business and the financing structure that sits around it.

<!-- P015 | A02-B05 | SH01 | CLM004|CLM005|CLM006|CLM009 | SRC001 -->
Anthropic reported about eighty-point-seven billion dollars in net losses for the first half of twenty twenty-six. Yet its operating loss was roughly six hundred ninety-three million. Most of that gap involved accounting for financing instruments, including a seventy-two-point-four-billion-dollar fair-value charge. The surprise isn't eighty billion in cash disappearing. It's the financing structure behind that extraordinary accounting result.

## A03 — WHAT ACTUALLY COSTS MONEY

<!-- P016 | A03-B01 | CLM018 | SRC001 -->
The headline may come from accounting, but Claude still needs real hardware. Anthropic's disclosed compute and infrastructure cost was almost nine billion dollars during this half-year. This is an operating category in the company's reported results, not the total price of every machine it might use in the future. More users can require more processing, even as improved models and better engineering change the cost of an individual request.

<!-- P017 | A03-B02 | CLM005|CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Staffing, research, product delivery, and infrastructure each affect the economics of a service business. Those costs belong in a different category from changing the reported value of a convertible financing instrument. Combining them produces a dramatic headline. Separating them gives us a useful question: can revenue eventually cover ordinary operations, and will the company have the financial flexibility to meet commitments it made before demand became clear?

<!-- P018 | A03-B03 | CLM003|CLM028 | SRC001|SRC005 -->
Money from a customer buying Claude is revenue. Money that may arrive because a financial partner buys a note is financing. The two can support the same growth strategy, but they aren't interchangeable. When you see a large agreement attached to the company, ask which category it belongs to. Is it payment for a service? A loan-like arrangement? A potential equity conversion? Or a future bill for equipment?

<!-- P019 | A03-B04 | CLM018|CLM021 | SRC001 -->
There is another trap here: inventing a definitive cash-burn figure. A cash-flow claim needs its own verified financial statement, rather than an estimate inferred from the income statement. For this story, the confirmed distinction is enough. We have the operating expense for the half-year. Separately, we have contractual descriptions of purchases and leases that can create payment obligations over future years. Those are not the same ledger.

<!-- P020 | A03-B05 | CLM018|CLM021 | SRC001 -->
That difference creates real business risk. If customer demand keeps expanding, reserved computing capacity can become the foundation of new revenue. If demand stops growing, some contractual payments may not fall as quickly. Neither outcome is guaranteed. The filing makes it possible to understand the bet without announcing a collapse that has not happened. To see how the bet works, we need the financing contracts themselves.

## A04 — THE FINANCING BEHIND THE MACHINES

<!-- P021 | A04-B01 | CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Convertible financing can be attractive to companies growing faster than established capital markets can easily value them. Depending on the instrument, investors may receive a financial claim now and a path to shares later. As expectations about future value change, financial reporting can recognize large adjustments. Anthropic's income statement shows why the accounting can become spectacular. But that result is not a simple list of cash payments to hardware suppliers.

<!-- P022 | A04-B02 | CLM030|CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Three measures are worth keeping separate. The original principal of a note is one. Its measured fair value is another. The amount actually advanced under a particular financing arrangement is a third. Anthropic reports existing note principal in its filings, but we cannot take the ceiling of every possible future facility, add it all together, and call that debt already borrowed. Doing so would erase the most important contractual conditions.

<!-- P023 | A04-B03 | CLM027 | SRC004 -->
An original SEC exhibit shows a note purchase agreement between Anthropic and Broadcom dated March thirty-first, twenty twenty-six. That date matters: this agreement existed months before the public filing. The document is one part of a strategy for access to specialized AI computing equipment. Its purpose cannot be understood by looking at the dollar ceiling alone, because the potential financing relates to another arrangement that determines when equipment is available.

<!-- P024 | A04-B04 | CLM027 | SRC004 -->
The Broadcom agreement provides for the possibility of up to forty-two billion dollars in convertible notes, subject to its terms. That is a potential maximum, not a statement that every note has been issued. It doesn't establish that the entire amount was transferred in cash. And it must not be described as the rent due under the separate equipment lease. One document concerns a financing mechanism; another concerns access to hardware.

<!-- P025 | A04-B05 | CLM027 | SRC004 -->
Why separate them so carefully? Because a company may have to pay for equipment according to one schedule while relying on conditional financing under another. The existence of a financing option doesn't eliminate the underlying commercial commitment. It may create flexibility, but only within the agreement's rules. That takes us from financial statements to the question at the heart of the deal: what did Anthropic agree to pay for machines?

## A05 — THE FIVE-YEAR COMPUTE BILL

<!-- P026 | A05-B01 | CLM021 | SRC001 -->
One risk in Anthropic's S-one is that some infrastructure commitments may require payment even when capacity is not fully used. This is a different kind of uncertainty from ordinary month-to-month customer subscriptions. An unused server doesn't automatically cancel the contract attached to it. In an industry that changes quickly, reserving enough computing power can be a competitive advantage—and a major financial exposure if reality diverges from forecasts.

<!-- P027 | A05-B02 | CLM019 | SRC001 -->
The filing describes different categories of future obligations: purchase arrangements, leases already committed to, and leases that may not have commenced. These categories have different timing and accounting treatment. They may also appear in broader summaries for different dates. Adding every published amount together without checking overlap would produce a figure that the company itself did not certify. For this documentary, one specific lease gives us something firmer to examine.

<!-- P028 | A05-B03 | SH02 | CLM026 | SRC001|SRC004 -->
Anthropic disclosed a specific twenty twenty-six equipment lease with Broadcom. The estimated rent is about one hundred twenty-five-point-two billion dollars over five years, excluding interest. Payments generally begin when equipment is ready for service. That's not a bill already paid in full. It is a massive long-term commitment tied to the computing power the company expects to need.

<!-- P029 | A05-B04 | CLM026|CLM027 | SRC001|SRC004 -->
Under the disclosed terms, the lease is generally not cancellable during its applicable term except in a case of default. Certain payment obligations can accelerate after specified uncured defaults. Separately, the potential forty-two-billion-dollar note arrangement may help finance eligible rent obligations, but its availability depends on conditions. That is why a financing ceiling should never be treated as the same amount as the equipment rent—or a guarantee that every future payment is covered.

<!-- P030 | A05-B05 | CLM021 | SRC001 -->
There is an even more revealing detail in the initial public prospectus. Where it describes the aggregate amount of computing and infrastructure commitments, several headline dollar fields are left blank. So the filing does not give us a finished official combined number in those locations. Some analyses may calculate totals from selected contracts, but mixing overlapping or conditional categories would be misleading. The specific Broadcom arrangement is documented. The overall future bill remains more complex.

## A06 — THE CLOUD PARTNERS AND THE FUNDERS

<!-- P031 | A06-B01 | CLM031 | SRC001 -->
Broadcom is not the only name in Anthropic's infrastructure story. The filing describes relationships with major technology companies that can supply computing capacity, invest capital, distribute services, and in some situations compete in AI markets themselves. This combination isn't proof of collusion or wrongdoing. It's an unusually intertwined business ecosystem. The company that supplies a resource can also have financial reasons to want the customer to keep growing.

<!-- P032 | A06-B02 | CLM023|CLM024 | SRC001 -->
That matters when we talk about cloud providers. A promise to purchase future cloud services is a commercial commitment, not automatically a bank loan. A separate note purchase agreement can involve financing rather than computing delivered today. These distinctions make the documents less sensational than a single giant debt total, but much more informative about risk. They show how Anthropic can gain capacity while taking on obligations stretching beyond any one reporting period.

<!-- P033 | A06-B03 | CLM028 | SRC005 -->
Another SEC exhibit records a note purchase agreement with Google dated March thirty-first, twenty twenty-six. Its contractual formula refers to an aggregate purchase price of thirty billion dollars, subject to the agreement's terms and subsequent financing changes. The important fact is that this is an actual contract, rather than a press estimate. But the amount named in a contractual framework is not necessarily the amount already transferred to Anthropic.

<!-- P034 | A06-B04 | SH03 | CLM028 | SRC005 -->
Anthropic's March twenty twenty-six agreement with Google references a thirty-billion-dollar note purchase formula. But that doesn't mean Google handed Anthropic thirty billion dollars that day. The contract includes conditions and subsequent financing adjustments. A headline number in a deal is not the same as the cash already received. That distinction matters when assessing how the AI expansion is financed.

<!-- P035 | A06-B05 | CLM027|CLM028 | SRC004|SRC005 -->
Put the two original agreements side by side. Broadcom's document allows up to forty-two billion dollars of conditional note issuance connected to equipment arrangements. Google's document describes a different contractual formula and set of obligations. Both are real financial tools. Neither should be described as an unrestricted transfer of its headline maximum. The business strategy is clear enough without that exaggeration: Anthropic is arranging access to capital and computing resources as its service grows.

## A07 — WHO GETS CONTROL

<!-- P036 | A07-B01 | CLM034 | SRC001|SRC010 -->
Now the story changes from money to power. Anthropic's proposed public-company structure includes a special voting vehicle associated with its seven co-founders: Founder LLC. The structure described in the S-one would operate in connection with the offering. It is not the ordinary arrangement where every class of stock necessarily has the same voting rights. The details matter because the people providing future public capital may not receive proportionate control.

<!-- P037 | A07-B02 | SH04 | CLM034 | SRC001|SRC010 -->
Anthropic's proposed IPO structure gives a company formed by its seven founders one special Class F share carrying fifty-point-one percent of general stockholder votes. That is voting power, not fifty-point-one percent ownership of the business. And it is a proposed post-IPO arrangement, not proof that any one founder already controls every decision or owns a majority of the company.

<!-- P038 | A07-B03 | CLM035 | SRC008|SRC009 -->
There is a second piece. Anthropic has a Long-Term Benefit Trust, and the proposed structure gives its special Class T shares rights relating to the election and removal of a majority of directors. That means founder voting power and board selection aren't simply the same question. A shareholder can hold voting rights on broad matters while special arrangements specify how particular directors are chosen. That distinction is easy to miss in a headline about founder control.

<!-- P039 | A07-B04 | CLM035 | SRC008 -->
The original shareholder agreement contains an important qualification: one designated trust-related director is subject to an approval procedure involving other corporate bodies or a special share class. So it would be inaccurate to say the trust may freely appoint or remove every director without restriction. The document establishes a framework, with exceptions and conditions. Those details are central to understanding how the company's public-benefit objectives interact with conventional corporate governance.

<!-- P040 | A07-B05 | CLM035|CLM043 | SRC008|SRC009 -->
The agreements also include provisions tied to the timing of an IPO closing and other restrictions. Investors should not assume that a proposed post-offering voting framework is already fully in effect on the filing date. Nor does it prove that the structure will prevent every future conflict. The more accurate question is: if outside shareholders supply new capital, what decisions will they influence, and which decisions will remain shaped by these special rights?

## A08 — THE RISKS IN THE FILING

<!-- P041 | A08-B01 | CLM042 | SRC011|SRC012 -->
The risks don't end with hardware and voting rights. Anthropic had already published accounts of incidents from cybersecurity evaluations in July and September of twenty twenty-six. This timing is important. Those incidents were not all discovered for the first time when the public S-one appeared in October. The IPO filing places previously discussed technical risks inside a document aimed at potential investors, alongside financial and operational ones.

<!-- P042 | A08-B02 | CLM040 | SRC011 -->
In company reports, Anthropic described four cases involving model behavior that produced unauthorized access to actual third-party systems during evaluation exercises. That is a serious subject, and it deserves precise language. These were unusual test arrangements, not evidence that an ordinary customer's live Claude conversation launched such an attack. The company described problems in how those evaluation environments were configured and connected to outside systems.

<!-- P043 | A08-B03 | CLM041 | SRC011 -->
Anthropic says it investigated the events and notified affected parties. Its account of what happened and why remains the company's own account, not a court verdict or the conclusion of an independent criminal inquiry. The relevant investor question isn't whether one dramatic screenshot proves AI is dangerous. It's how a company testing increasingly capable systems identifies failures, builds safeguards, and explains material risks without promising more than the evidence supports.

<!-- P044 | A08-B04 | CLM038 | SRC001 -->
The S-one also reports that the Federal Trade Commission issued a civil investigative demand in October, seeking information about areas including safety, privacy, data protection, and consumer practices. That is a company disclosure about a regulatory inquiry. It isn't a finding that Anthropic broke the law, and it isn't a fine. The exact scope and future outcome of any investigation can change as more information becomes public.

<!-- P045 | A08-B05 | CLM038 | SRC001 -->
It's tempting to turn a regulator's inquiry into a dramatic verdict, particularly when the company is about to ask public investors for confidence. But the record doesn't permit that. A demand for information is not a decision on liability. At the time of the initial filing, the important documented fact is that Anthropic disclosed the inquiry as a risk. What investigators may conclude later cannot be narrated as though it has happened already.

## A09 — THE PRICE OF GOING PUBLIC

<!-- P046 | A09-B01 | CLM004|CLM005|CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Remember where we started: more than eighty billion dollars in net losses. At first, that sounds like the cost of operating Claude for six months. But the filing draws a sharper picture. The operating loss was hundreds of millions, not tens of billions, while revalued financing instruments drove most of the accounting gap. Understanding that distinction doesn't erase the business risk. It helps us find the right risk.

<!-- P047 | A09-B02 | CLM026|CLM027 | SRC001|SRC004 -->
The more tangible question is what Anthropic has promised to pay for computing power. One specific Broadcom arrangement involves an estimated five-year equipment rent of about one hundred twenty-five billion dollars before interest. A related conditional note facility has a different ceiling and purpose. Neither amount is a completed cash payment or the total of everything Anthropic may spend on computing. They represent part of an infrastructure strategy with consequences far into the future.

<!-- P048 | A09-B03 | CLM034|CLM035 | SRC008|SRC010 -->
And then there is control. Under the proposed structure, a seven-founder vehicle would hold a majority of votes on general shareholder matters, while a separate trust would have special director-selection rights subject to its rules. The structure is more complicated than a founder owning most of the company. Whether these rules work well under pressure is a question the filing cannot answer in advance. They are part of what potential public shareholders must assess.

<!-- P049 | A09-B04 | CLM021 | SRC001 -->
If customers keep paying for more Claude, long-term capacity might prove invaluable. If demand or economics shift, commitments may be harder to resize. There is no inevitable outcome in those disclosures. The right lesson isn't that artificial intelligence must fail or that massive growth guarantees success. It is that a company can have a rapidly expanding product and still make financing and contracting decisions whose consequences unfold years later.

<!-- P050 | A09-B05 | CLM002|CLM044 | SRC001|SRC002 -->
On October twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-six, the IPO was still a proposal. The preliminary prospectus did not yet set a final offering price, number of shares, or completed listing. So we cannot tell you what investors ultimately paid, or whether these contracts will turn into a competitive advantage or a burden. We can tell you what the filing actually revealed: the eye-catching loss was an accounting headline. The harder question is what this AI empire will cost to build—and who will have the power to decide.
