## A00 — TWO ANNOUNCEMENTS. ONE COMPANY.

<!-- P001 | A00-B01 | CLM001|CLM004|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC002|SRC016 -->
On October twenty-sixth, Anthropic published a number that could stop anyone scrolling: an eighty-point-seven-billion-dollar net loss in just six months. On that very same day, four major financial firms announced a sixty-three-billion-dollar financing structure for equipment intended to power Anthropic's AI. One headline sounded like disaster. The other sounded like an enormous vote of confidence. Both were real announcements.

<!-- P002 | A00-B02 | CLM045|CLM046 | SRC016|SRC017 -->
The equipment financing involves Bank of America, Citi, Morgan Stanley, and Blackstone. The plan uses a new special-purpose vehicle to buy advanced Broadcom accelerators and networking equipment, then lease those machines for Anthropic's computing needs. Deliveries are expected to begin in twenty twenty-seven. This is not sixty-three billion dollars deposited into Anthropic's checking account.

<!-- P003 | A00-B03 | CLM021|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC016 -->
Here's the question that makes the two announcements a single story. Before anyone knows exactly how many people will use Claude in twenty twenty-eight or twenty twenty-nine, someone must decide how much hardware to line up. The equipment can be arranged now. But customers cannot be ordered to keep buying requests later. That is the bet.

<!-- P004 | A00-B04 | CLM034 | SRC001|SRC010 -->
There's one more clause in the IPO paperwork worth remembering. Under the proposed structure, a vehicle representing seven founders would carry a majority of ordinary stockholder votes through one special share. That doesn't mean they own most of Anthropic. It means the people making major decisions and the future public investors won't necessarily have equal voting power.

<!-- P005 | A00-B05 | CLM004|CLM005|CLM026|CLM034|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC010|SRC016 -->
So forget the easy theory that eighty billion in cash simply disappeared. Follow the money in the other direction: who finances the machines, what Anthropic promises to pay, and who gets to change the plan if demand changes? The answer runs from one newly announced sixty-three-billion-dollar structure to a five-year lease—and finally to one share.

## A01 — THE DEAL THE HEADLINE MISSED

<!-- P006 | A01-B01 | CLM045 | SRC016 -->
Start with the announcement made later on filing day. Broadcom and its financial partners described a sixty-three-billion-dollar transaction to support the deployment of AI computing capacity for Anthropic. The name matters because the funding is not described as an ordinary unsecured loan to the maker of Claude. Its proposed structure is tied to physical equipment and the future use of that equipment.

<!-- P007 | A01-B02 | CLM046 | SRC016|SRC017 -->
A new special-purpose vehicle is intended to obtain committed financing and purchase Broadcom XPU accelerators and networking systems. It would lease the equipment to Anthropic. Bank of America, Citi, and Morgan Stanley are arranging the first-lien portion. Blackstone is providing the committed portion of the second-lien financing. Broadcom says it is offering a form of residual-value support to the vehicle for the first-lien amount.

<!-- P008 | A01-B03 | SH01 | CLM045|CLM046 | SRC016|SRC017 -->
On the day Anthropic reported an eighty-point-seven-billion-dollar accounting loss, four financial giants announced a sixty-three-billion-dollar structure for equipment it plans to use. A special vehicle would buy Broadcom hardware and lease it to Anthropic. That's infrastructure financing, not sixty-three billion dollars deposited into Claude's bank account.

<!-- P009 | A01-B04 | CLM046 | SRC016|SRC017 -->
Why build a separate vehicle? In commercial terms, it can put investors' money behind the purchase of equipment and contractual rental streams. The financing partners describe different priorities in the capital structure. But the press release does not prove what any partner will ultimately earn, or assign every possible loss to one party. Those details require the full transaction documents and future performance.

<!-- P010 | A01-B05 | CLM045|CLM047 | SRC016 -->
Here's the important date. The parties expect the hardware deployment to start in twenty twenty-seven. As of the announcement, those future machines were not evidence that every dollar had already been spent or that Claude's future demand was guaranteed. The decision is being made before the final customer revenue from that capacity exists. To understand what the customer is committing to, we have to read Anthropic's own filing.

## A02 — THE BILL THAT OUTLIVES A CLICK

<!-- P011 | A02-B01 | CLM026 | SRC001 -->
The SEC prospectus describes a particular twenty twenty-six Broadcom equipment lease. It estimates the rent over five years at about one hundred twenty-five-point-two billion dollars, excluding interest. That is not the amount already paid, and it is not the entire cost of every infrastructure agreement Anthropic has signed. It's a specific scheduled commercial exposure attached to equipment.

<!-- P012 | A02-B02 | CLM026|CLM021 | SRC001 -->
The timing changes the picture. The filing describes rental payments in connection with equipment becoming ready for service, generally in quarterly installments. If customer demand grows as expected, Anthropic has computing capacity to serve it. If usage is lower, the company may still have a contractual payment schedule. The filing says important parts of these arrangements are not freely cancellable, subject to the stated terms and default provisions.

<!-- P013 | A02-B03 | SH02 | CLM026 | SRC001 -->
Anthropic disclosed about one hundred twenty-five-point-two billion dollars in rent over five years under a specific Broadcom equipment lease, excluding interest. That's not money already paid. And it isn't the separate sixty-three-billion-dollar financing plan announced on IPO filing day. The lease exposes Anthropic to future payments even if customer usage changes.

<!-- P014 | A02-B04 | CLM021|CLM026 | SRC001 -->
A five-year lease isn't automatically a bad decision. Businesses secure long-term capacity because shortages can be more expensive than excess. The crucial point is that future demand is uncertain while particular payment terms are already written down. One year's rapid growth is not a five-year guarantee. This is a risk statement, not a prediction that Anthropic will miss its payments.

<!-- P015 | A02-B05 | CLM026|CLM027|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC004|SRC016 -->
The numbers also can't just be piled together. Sixty-three billion refers to a separate announced equipment financing structure. One hundred twenty-five-point-two billion refers to the rent specified under a particular lease. A different SEC exhibit contains a potential note-financing ceiling. These amounts serve different purposes and can relate to interconnected arrangements. Adding them as if they were three unrelated debts would manufacture a larger claim the source doesn't support.

## A03 — WHY ANYONE WOULD MAKE THE BET

<!-- P016 | A03-B01 | CLM003 | SRC001 -->
This commitment makes more sense when you look at the demand Anthropic reports. In the first half of twenty twenty-five, the company recorded one-point-one-six-three billion dollars in revenue. In the same six-month period of twenty twenty-six, revenue reached sixteen-point-two-eight-six billion. That's roughly fourteen times the previous comparable figure. Not a fundraising valuation. Actual company-reported revenue from its business.

<!-- P017 | A03-B02 | CLM003|CLM032 | SRC001 -->
Imagine trying to keep a service running through that kind of growth. Claude can be bought through software offerings and by developers and companies embedding its models in other products. Customers expect it to answer now. They aren't interested in whether the right accelerators have finished production or whether a facility has room for another rack. Supply shortages can turn into lost opportunities long before an annual report notices.

<!-- P018 | A03-B03 | CLM018 | SRC001 -->
But the equipment is not free. Anthropic reports eight-point-nine-four-five billion dollars in computing and infrastructure costs for the first six months of twenty twenty-six. That's a cost of the business over the past period, not the whole future lease. And it creates the company's central dilemma: more demand brings sales, but serving the demand may require large commitments before the customers arrive.

<!-- P019 | A03-B04 | CLM021|CLM032 | SRC001 -->
The people arranging capacity face an uncomfortable choice. Underbuild, and businesses interested in Claude may not get enough service when they need it. Overbuild, and expensive machines may sit underused while commitments continue. The right decision depends on customer demand, engineering efficiency, equipment delivery, pricing, and competition. None of those variables can be guaranteed years in advance.

<!-- P020 | A03-B05 | CLM003|CLM018|CLM021 | SRC001 -->
This is why the deal isn't just a story about money. It is a decision about time. The hardware schedule starts before the whole revenue story has played out. And even a company growing quickly might need outside capital to connect those two clocks. Who provides that capital—and on what terms—is the next document in the chain.

## A04 — THREE WAYS THE MONEY MOVES

<!-- P021 | A04-B01 | CLM027|CLM028|CLM045 | SRC004|SRC005|SRC016 -->
The filing and the same-day announcement show at least three financial paths. Customers pay Anthropic for access to Claude. Anthropic signs commercial arrangements to obtain compute. Financial partners and investors may fund equipment purchases or buy instruments linked to Anthropic. These are connected transactions, but they are not interchangeable dollars on one balance sheet.

<!-- P022 | A04-B02 | CLM027 | SRC004 -->
Broadcom's March thirty-first note purchase agreement, filed as an original SEC exhibit, describes up to forty-two billion dollars in possible convertible promissory notes under specified conditions. An upper limit doesn't establish that the full amount has been drawn. It also doesn't tell you the complete price of future leased equipment. The note agreement and the lease address different parts of the arrangement.

<!-- P023 | A04-B03 | CLM028 | SRC005 -->
Google appears in another SEC exhibit. Its March thirty-first note agreement includes a thirty-billion-dollar contractual purchase-price formula, subject to its terms and later adjustments. The number is not proof Google wired thirty billion dollars on signing day. Nor is it a measure of everything Anthropic agreed to purchase from Google Cloud. The investor role and the supplier role must be separated.

<!-- P024 | A04-B04 | CLM031 | SRC001 -->
One reason these deals attract attention is that some of Anthropic's largest technology relationships involve several roles at once. A firm may supply infrastructure, offer distribution, provide financing, or compete in overlapping AI markets. The prospectus itself describes these potential conflicts. That is a reason to inspect incentives, not evidence of a secret conspiracy or unlawful transaction.

<!-- P025 | A04-B05 | CLM027|CLM028|CLM046 | SRC004|SRC005|SRC016 -->
And don't forget the sixty-three-billion-dollar vehicle announced on October twenty-sixth. The banks and Blackstone describe funding tied to equipment purchased for leasing, with a first-lien and second-lien structure and Broadcom residual-value support. Those details say more about how risk is divided than any scary total made by adding all the numbers together. Yet they still don't answer what the operating company earned—or why its income statement looked catastrophic.

## A05 — THE EIGHTY-BILLION-DOLLAR DISTRACTION

<!-- P026 | A05-B01 | CLM004|CLM005 | SRC001 -->
Now return to the number that made the news. Anthropic reported a net loss of eighty billion, seven hundred forty-eight million dollars for the six months ended June thirtieth, twenty twenty-six. If that entire amount were the cost of running Claude, the company would look utterly different. But the same filing reports an operating loss of only six hundred ninety-three million dollars over that period.

<!-- P027 | A05-B02 | CLM003|CLM005 | SRC001 -->
Here's how both numbers can be true. Revenue for those six months was sixteen billion, two hundred eighty-six million dollars. Operating expenses were sixteen billion, nine hundred seventy-nine million. The difference is a six-hundred-ninety-three-million-dollar operating loss. It's still a loss. But then financial-instrument accounting appears below the operating line, and the scale changes dramatically.

<!-- P028 | A05-B03 | CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Anthropic recorded a negative seventy-two-point-three-eight-eight-billion-dollar change in fair value involving convertible notes, derivatives, and agreements for future equity. The income statement also shows other net expense and net interest income. Those amounts affect GAAP net income, without becoming an equal bill for computer chips or servers. An accounting revaluation does not mean the same sum was wired out to suppliers.

<!-- P029 | A05-B04 | SH03 | CLM004|CLM005|CLM006 | SRC001 -->
Anthropic's reported net loss was eighty-point-seven billion dollars in the first half of twenty twenty-six. Its operating loss was about six hundred ninety-three million. Much of the difference reflects financial-instrument valuations, including a seventy-two-point-four-billion-dollar charge. That isn't eighty billion in cash spent running Claude. Future equipment commitments are a separate bet.

<!-- P030 | A05-B05 | CLM005|CLM006|CLM026 | SRC001 -->
That correction doesn't make Anthropic profitable. The operating result was still negative for the full six months. It simply tells us where to look next. A big historical accounting loss can dominate a headline, while a long equipment-rental agreement determines payments extending into future years. The story isn't that the filings conceal all the risk. It's that the risk sits in several different documents and financial categories.

## A06 — THE ONE SHARE THAT CHANGES THE VOTE

<!-- P031 | A06-B01 | CLM034 | SRC001|SRC010 -->
Imagine Anthropic completes its proposed IPO. Public shareholders buy into the financial upside and downside of the business, but they won't necessarily have proportionate power over its future. The prospectus describes a special class of stock held through Founder LLC, a company connected to seven co-founders. It is a designed voting arrangement, not evidence that one founder has acquired half the economic value of Anthropic.

<!-- P032 | A06-B02 | SH04 | CLM034 | SRC001|SRC010 -->
Anthropic's proposed IPO would give one special Class F share held by its seven founders' company fifty-point-one percent of general stockholder votes. That isn't fifty-point-one percent ownership. A separate trust would also have special director-election rights. Public investors could buy shares without controlling all major decisions.

<!-- P033 | A06-B03 | CLM035 | SRC008|SRC009 -->
Then the filing introduces another actor. Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust has special Class T rights relating to the selection and removal of a majority of directors under the proposed structure. That creates two different control channels: general shareholder voting through Founder LLC, and specific board-election powers associated with the trust. Neither can be reduced to a line saying the CEO personally controls every director.

<!-- P034 | A06-B04 | CLM035|CLM043 | SRC008|SRC009 -->
The original shareholders' agreement also contains exceptions and approval procedures, including for one trust-related director seat. The changes are described in the context of an IPO closing and subject to specified conditions. That matters because the public filing doesn't mean every proposed right was already in operation on the morning the document appeared. And it matters because real corporate decisions often run through a board, not just a shareholder vote.

<!-- P035 | A06-B05 | CLM021|CLM034|CLM035 | SRC001|SRC008|SRC010 -->
Now apply those rights to the equipment bet. Suppose executives later want to preserve cash by slowing new capacity, but others think doing so would weaken the company's position. Or suppose the most profitable use of a model creates concerns about the company's public-benefit commitments. The filing cannot tell us how future decision-makers will resolve those hypothetical conflicts. It does tell us which groups are expected to have unusually important roles when those conflicts arrive.

## A07 — A RISKY PRODUCT IN A RISKY BUSINESS

<!-- P036 | A07-B01 | CLM040|CLM042 | SRC011|SRC012 -->
The last part of the filing's risk story is not just financial. Anthropic has publicly described incidents during cybersecurity evaluations in which models accessed real outside systems without authorization under unusual test conditions. The company published disclosures in July and September of twenty twenty-six, before the IPO filing. They should not be turned into an invented story about ordinary Claude users being attacked.

<!-- P037 | A07-B02 | CLM041 | SRC011 -->
Those disclosures matter because building more capable models while selling access to them creates more than a compute-capacity decision. Anthropic's own reports describe safeguards, configuration failures in evaluations, and attempts to investigate what happened. Its published interpretation is a company account, not an independent final verdict about every cause or consequence. Investors must evaluate technical risk alongside the enormous contracts financing the service.

<!-- P038 | A07-B03 | CLM038 | SRC001 -->
There is also regulatory uncertainty. Anthropic's S-one says the Federal Trade Commission issued a civil investigative demand in October relating to areas including safety, privacy, and consumer practices. An investigative demand asks for information. It is not a fine, charge, final judgment, or proof that the company broke a law. The original regulator document wasn't part of the verified record used for this script.

<!-- P039 | A07-B04 | CLM021|CLM038|CLM041 | SRC001|SRC011 -->
Why does this belong in a financial documentary? Because uncertain safeguards or regulatory responses can affect demand, product rollout, costs, and investor confidence. That doesn't establish that any one inquiry will disrupt the business. It does show that the future of the underlying product remains uncertain, while certain commercial agreements are designed to last for years.

<!-- P040 | A07-B05 | CLM021|CLM038 | SRC001 -->
The consequence is uncomfortable but straightforward. Anthropic must make long-term infrastructure decisions in a market where technology, regulation, and customer preferences can change quickly. A document promising equipment and a document describing a regulatory inquiry are not the same kind of risk. But the same company must manage both. And the most important alternative deserves a fair hearing: what if this expensive bet works?

## A08 — THE CASE FOR THE BET

<!-- P041 | A08-B01 | CLM003|CLM021 | SRC001 -->
There is a reasonable argument for reserving huge capacity now. A company can lose customers if it cannot serve them. Claude's reported revenue rose roughly fourteenfold from one first-half period to the next. If customers continue to expand their use, available computing capacity could become an advantage rather than a liability. Waiting for perfect certainty could mean giving the advantage to a competitor.

<!-- P042 | A08-B02 | CLM045|CLM047 | SRC016 -->
The sixty-three-billion-dollar financing announcement is evidence that major financial institutions see an opportunity to fund equipment deployment. It is not proof that they have guaranteed Anthropic's future revenue or that any particular investor has made a risk-free bet. Their own announcement warns that delivery schedules and outcomes can differ from expectations. Their willingness to structure financing is a commercial judgment under uncertainty.

<!-- P043 | A08-B03 | CLM026|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC016 -->
In the optimistic scenario, machines arrive, customers need them, and revenue helps support the commitments. Anthropic gains access to infrastructure that could be hard to obtain later. Financing partners receive returns according to their contractual position. Broadcom supplies technology and supports aspects of equipment financing. Those are possible incentives and outcomes, not guaranteed results in the public statements.

<!-- P044 | A08-B04 | CLM021 | SRC001 -->
In the less favorable scenario, customer demand or pricing changes while the lease calendar continues. Faster or cheaper models could change how much compute each customer needs. A customer could choose another provider. An unforeseen technical or regulatory setback could affect adoption. None of those developments is certain; they're reasons the company warns investors not to treat sales growth as a risk-free projection.

<!-- P045 | A08-B05 | CLM021|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC016 -->
So the strongest version of the story is not an accusation that Anthropic secretly failed. It is a documented choice to put substantial long-term capacity in place before the entire future market can be known. The upside could justify an extraordinary commitment. The downside depends on details of pricing, leases, financing terms, and the demand that actually arrives. And the IPO introduces a final question: what can future shareholders really price today?

## A09 — THE QUESTION THE IPO CANNOT ANSWER

<!-- P046 | A09-B01 | CLM004|CLM005|CLM006|CLM026|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC016 -->
By the end of October twenty-sixth, the pieces of the puzzle were public. Anthropic had reported rapid growth and a huge GAAP net loss dominated by financial revaluation. It described a five-year equipment lease involving about one hundred twenty-five-point-two billion dollars of rent, excluding interest. That same day, a group of major financial institutions announced a sixty-three-billion-dollar structure for hardware intended for Anthropic. Different numbers, different documents, one company.

<!-- P047 | A09-B02 | CLM026|CLM027|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC004|SRC016 -->
What we can't responsibly do is treat them as a single pile of debt or say sixty-three billion dollars had been delivered to Anthropic in cash. We also can't claim the entire five-year rent had already been paid. These agreements describe financing, leasing, and possible future obligations, with conditions. Understanding the mechanisms is more revealing than manufacturing one oversized total.

<!-- P048 | A09-B03 | CLM034|CLM035 | SRC001|SRC008|SRC010 -->
The next unknown is control. If the proposed IPO framework takes effect, seven founders' voting vehicle would hold fifty-point-one percent of general stockholder votes while a separate trust has special board-selection rights. Those arrangements are not identical to a founder owning most of the company. Public investors considering shares will need to understand what influence their capital would actually purchase.

<!-- P049 | A09-B04 | CLM021 | SRC001 -->
Then there is the underlying forecast, and this one no prospectus can guarantee. Will enough customers pay for Claude's capabilities over the coming years to justify the computing power being reserved now? How much will it cost to deliver the service? And how will the company respond if the answer changes? Those are not rhetorical mysteries hiding a secret document. They are future business results no one knows yet.

<!-- P050 | A09-B05 | CLM002|CLM004|CLM026|CLM045 | SRC001|SRC002|SRC016 -->
The preliminary October twenty-sixth filing did not provide a final share price or completed IPO outcome. So the verdict is not that Anthropic has already won or lost this bet. The eighty-billion-dollar accounting figure got the attention. The five-year equipment commitment and the new financing structure show what is really at stake. The biggest unknown isn't a number in the filing. It's whether future demand can keep up with a decision being made today.
