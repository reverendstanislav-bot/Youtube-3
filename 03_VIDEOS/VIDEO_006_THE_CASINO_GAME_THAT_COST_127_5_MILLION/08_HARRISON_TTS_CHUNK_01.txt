Light & Wonder had signed the deal keeping it off the market. The evidence trail led to Excel spreadsheets.

Light & Wonder paid Aristocrat one hundred and twenty-seven point five million dollars to settle Dragon Train. But the deal also barred commercialization of Dragon Train and Jewel of the Dragon worldwide—including future games in both families. Not a criminal fine. Not a jury award.

In a preliminary ruling, the judge described matching spreadsheet oddities: borders around empty rows, gray-filled cells, even identical formatting errors. Then came another clue: a file creation date. November twelfth, twenty thirteen. Years before Dragon Train reached the public.

How did an old timestamp become evidence? What did those files say about the game's hidden math? And why did the games stay off the market even after the court's ban vanished?

A slot-machine screen shows symbols and prizes. A development worksheet can show how those outcomes were designed. That difference made the records behind Dragon Train important.

Before Dragon Train reached players, a Dragon Link math worksheet was shared inside Light & Wonder. Aristocrat pointed to that September 2022 email in its trade-secret case. A federal judge later examined it in a preliminary ruling. The issue wasn't merely similar symbols on two screens: an earlier rival's internal worksheet had become part of the evidence.

The court examined specifics: how certain bonus symbols were assigned prizes and how major-jackpot probabilities were calculated. Those details made a stronger question than whether both games featured dragons.

Similarity alone still wasn't proof that everything was secret. The judge had to separate what a player could infer from what a designer's confidential files might reveal.
