## A00

Light & Wonder's Dragon Train sparked a court fight with Aristocrat. Two game families would lose their commercial future.

The crucial clue wasn't onscreen. It was an old development spreadsheet.

Light & Wonder paid rival Aristocrat one hundred and twenty-seven point five million dollars. But money was only part of the settlement: it agreed to stop commercializing Dragon Train and Jewel of the Dragon worldwide, including future games in those families. Federal court papers confirmed the payment. This was not a jury damages award or criminal fine.

The judge's preliminary record would point back to a spreadsheet creation date from years before Dragon Train was shown publicly.

How could evidence buried in a development file help end the future sales of two game families?

## A01

A slot-machine screen shows symbols and prizes. A development worksheet can show how those outcomes were designed. That difference made the records behind Dragon Train important.

Before Dragon Train reached players, a Dragon Link math worksheet was shared inside Light & Wonder. Aristocrat pointed to that September 2022 email in its trade-secret case. A federal judge later examined it in a preliminary ruling. The issue wasn't merely similar symbols on two screens: an earlier rival's internal worksheet had become part of the evidence.

The court examined specifics: how certain bonus symbols were assigned prizes and how major-jackpot probabilities were calculated. Those details made a stronger question than whether both games featured dragons.

Similarity alone still wasn't proof that everything was secret. The judge had to separate what a player could infer from what a designer's confidential files might reveal.

To see why Aristocrat cared, go back to the games it made before Dragon Train existed.

## A02

Aristocrat introduced Lightning Link in 2014 and Dragon Link in 2016. The preliminary court order describes their complex development and Aristocrat's claim that the mathematical design was valuable—not just the artwork.

In the court's account, one mathematician linked the two companies. Emma Charles worked for Aristocrat until 2017. In July 2021, she joined Scientific Games, which later became Light & Wonder. Changing jobs was not the allegation. The dispute concerned how the new game's math was developed.

By late 2021, Charles was working on Dragon Train with other developers. Light & Wonder would later point to work done independently by its team. Aristocrat questioned whether experience from the earlier games had crossed into use of confidential mathematical material.

In August 2023, Light & Wonder presented Dragon Train at the Australasian Gaming Expo. The rival product was now on public display, even though the calculations behind its features remained out of view.

An Aristocrat designer played Dragon Train and noticed similarities with Dragon Link, the preliminary court ruling recounts. Familiar-looking features could raise a question. They couldn't, on their own, reveal the source of the rival game's math.

## A03

Aristocrat filed its federal lawsuit in Nevada in February 2024. It alleged trade-secret misuse and other intellectual-property violations. Light & Wonder disputed material allegations. At this point, neither side had a final jury verdict.

The September 2022 worksheet was one link in the paper trail described by the preliminary judge. The record also allowed a closer comparison between the rival development files.

The court looked beyond calculated results to the choices left on the sheets themselves: the kind of tiny layout decisions that a player would never see on a cabinet.

In the Aristocrat versus Light & Wonder lawsuit, a judge described strange similarities between game-development spreadsheets: borders around empty rows, the same gray-filled cells, even matching formatting errors. Those details mattered in tracing where the math might have originated. This was a preliminary ruling, not a final jury verdict.

Those formatting similarities gave the judge one route through the evidence. A different workbook, used during an early version of Dragon Train, opened another.

## A04

The early version was called Sky Train. The workbook was named Inception Conception, as identified in the preliminary court order.

The judge compared that workbook's creation metadata with an older Aristocrat Dragon Link file. The recorded date raised a question that visible gameplay couldn't answer.

A Light & Wonder development spreadsheet for an early Dragon Train version carried a creation date of November twelfth, 2013, according to a federal judge. It matched an older Aristocrat workbook's timestamp. The court treated that as a clue about origin, not a final verdict that every calculation in the rival game was copied.

The judge also examined how prizes were assigned and how certain jackpot probabilities were calculated. Those comparisons added weight to the question of where the early game's mathematics had come from.

No single timestamp decided the whole lawsuit. The judge weighed it alongside the spreadsheet similarities and the competing accounts of how the games had been developed.

## A05

Light & Wonder challenged the inference of copying. Its team, the company argued, had developed important parts of its own math; some features differed, and some statistical patterns could be worked out from public play.

A federal judge agreed with Light & Wonder on part of the Dragon Train math dispute. In a preliminary ruling, the court said certain bonus-symbol probabilities might be inferred from visible gameplay. But other mathematical combinations still raised serious trade-secret concerns. One successful defense argument didn't clear the company of the broader claim.

That limited win for Light & Wonder did not dispose of the worksheet evidence. The judge treated probabilities inferable from public play differently from disputed underlying mathematical combinations.

After weighing the evidence, the judge still considered Aristocrat extremely likely to succeed on key trade-secret issues at that preliminary stage. That assessment mattered immediately to the rival game.

The case had not reached a final damages trial. But the court did not have to wait for one before considering temporary restrictions on a product.

## A06

Now the debate had a commercial consequence. Could Dragon Train continue to be sold while a federal judge considered trade-secret protection before a final trial?

A federal judge temporarily restricted Light & Wonder's Dragon Train in September 2024, while Aristocrat's trade-secret case continued. Light & Wonder disagreed with the order. When the companies settled in 2026, that preliminary injunction was vacated. But the separate settlement agreement still stopped commercialization of the affected games.

For existing Dragon Train installations, the court also set a compliance-reporting deadline. But the available records don't establish a final number of machines physically removed around the world.

Light & Wonder publicly disagreed with the temporary order. It estimated that Dragon Train would account for less than five percent of its targeted 2025 adjusted earnings measure. That was the company's forecast—not a measured loss or the court's damages award.

And the restrictions would soon involve another game family.

## A07

The business problem was crossing borders. Related proceedings were underway in Australia, but the courts were considering different temporary remedies—not one worldwide verdict.

In February 2025, an Australian court refused the additional mandatory interim orders Aristocrat requested. Light & Wonder had already offered undertakings extending key U.S.-style restrictions there. That narrow procedural ruling did not disprove the underlying allegations.

In July 2025, the companies stipulated to extending the U.S. provisional restrictions to Jewel of the Dragon. What began with Dragon Train now involved two named game families.

For certain other Hold and Spin games, the eventual settlement provided a separate process for disputed mathematics. That was not a ruling that every Light & Wonder title had used Aristocrat's confidential information.

The decisive step was the shift from temporary courtroom restrictions to a negotiated long-term agreement. That agreement would decide the commercial future of the two named game families.

## A08

When the companies announced their deal in January 2026, it did more than establish a payment. It also governed which games could be commercialized, what mathematical information could be used, and what records had to be destroyed.

Light & Wonder acknowledged that certain mathematical information had been used to develop Dragon Train and Jewel of the Dragon. The signed court document described it as information Aristocrat contended was proprietary and confidential. That's a precise contractual statement, not a jury finding on every disputed element.

In February 2026, a federal court vacated temporary injunctions in Aristocrat's case against Light & Wonder. Yet Dragon Train and Jewel of the Dragon remained subject to permanent commercial restrictions. Why? Light & Wonder had agreed to those restrictions in its settlement contract. The court orders ended; the contractual promise survived.

The agreement covered land-based and digital commercialization of both game families. It also required an end to use of the covered math and destruction of related documents. For existing installations, the companies described a best-efforts removal commitment—not a verified count of units taken away.

The signed federal papers confirmed that Light & Wonder had paid Aristocrat $127.5 million. The company also reported roughly $128 million of settlement-related expense in its 2025 financial results. Those figures describe the same settlement, not two different payments to add together.

In February 2026, the U.S. claims and counterclaims were dismissed. According to Light & Wonder's SEC reporting, Aristocrat's claims against specified company entities in Australia were dismissed in March. That resolved the direct corporate dispute on agreed terms, but a different kind of lawsuit would follow.

## A09

The direct company-versus-company dispute was settled. But the story had a different legal afterlife.

Light & Wonder settled the Dragon Train dispute with Aristocrat. Then an entirely different lawsuit followed. In April 2026, investor plaintiff AztecGold alleged Light & Wonder misled Australian investors about that game. Light & Wonder disputed the allegations. Its August 2026 SEC filing reported no investor damages award. The original settlement had not ended every legal challenge.

The proposed class covered Australian-listed depositary-interest buyers from May 2023 through September 2024.

An August 2026 filing said Light & Wonder contested the claims; no verdict or quantified damages were reported.

The $127.5 million payment ended the direct corporate lawsuit. It didn't preserve the products. Under the settlement, Light & Wonder gave up future commercialization of Dragon Train, Jewel of the Dragon, and new games in either family.
