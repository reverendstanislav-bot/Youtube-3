**P001**

Two casino-game families. A $127.5 million settlement. And a clue hiding in a spreadsheet.

**P002**

## A01 — THE INVISIBLE PRODUCT

**P006**

Look at a casino machine from the player's side. You see animated symbols, features, and the possibility of a prize. What you don't see is the model determining how those events are arranged.

**P007** **[SHORT S02 — text/legal Stage04 pass; audio timing unverified]**

Two casino-game makers, Aristocrat and Light & Wonder, fought over what players couldn't see: game mathematics. The dispute centered on math used to build Dragon Train, not just the dragons onscreen. Some patterns can be inferred from gameplay. Other underlying combinations may be confidential. That difference mattered in court.

**P008**

Imagine two games with similar bonus features. You can observe how they behave, but observing a game is not the same as possessing its internal worksheets: the design choices and calculations behind those features. That information may have taken time, expertise, and money to develop.

**P009**

And not every probability is a secret. A player, mathematician, or rival designer might infer certain patterns from what the game publicly does. The court would later make exactly that distinction.

**P010**

So to understand the lawsuit, start with the earlier games that Aristocrat says carried its valuable mathematical work.

## A02 — THE PERSON AND THE LAUNCH

**P011**

Aristocrat developed Lightning Link, followed by Dragon Link. Those products helped establish the company's particular approach to game mechanics, prizes, and mathematical design. A competing manufacturer had every incentive to build something successful of its own.

**P012**

One person in the court's account connected those worlds. Mathematician Emma Charles worked for Aristocrat until 2017. In July 2021, she joined Scientific Games, the business that became Light & Wonder. Those employment dates are documented; changing employers, of course, is not itself wrongdoing.

**P013**

By late 2021, Charles was working on Dragon Train. Other designers and mathematicians were involved too. Light & Wonder would argue that its people developed important parts independently. For Aristocrat, the central question became whether the new team's work crossed the line from industry experience into confidential information.

**P014**

In August 2023, Light & Wonder presented Dragon Train at the Australasian Gaming Expo. A rival title was now in public view. What customers saw could be compared. The mathematical files used to develop it could not.

**P015**

According to the preliminary court decision, an Aristocrat designer played Dragon Train and immediately noticed similarities with Dragon Link. He could see shared-looking features, but not the math behind them. That gap between what was visible and what Aristocrat suspected would become the center of the dispute.

## A03 — FROM SIMILARITY TO A FILE

**P016**

In February 2024, Aristocrat sued in federal court in Nevada. Its allegations included trade-secret and other intellectual-property claims. Light & Wonder contested material allegations. At that stage, the accusations were arguments in a lawsuit, not findings made after a jury trial.

**P017**

One part of the judge's later ruling reached back to September 2022. According to the court's preliminary account, a Dragon Link base-game worksheet had been shared inside the Light & Wonder team during work described as a reverse-engineering training exercise. That mattered because the file was linked to Aristocrat's earlier game development.

**P018**

These weren't things a casino player could inspect. They were development materials: worksheets, calculations, and choices that could reflect how a game had been built. Aristocrat argued those private details mattered far more than surface resemblance. The judge examined their relationship, not just a single coincidental cell.

**P019** **[SHORT S03 — text/legal Stage04 pass; audio timing unverified]**

In the Aristocrat versus Light & Wonder lawsuit, a judge described strange similarities between game-development spreadsheets: borders around empty rows, the same gray-filled cells, even matching formatting errors. Those details mattered in tracing where the math might have originated. This was a preliminary ruling, not a final jury verdict.

**P020**

The question wasn't whether every spreadsheet contains boxes. They all do. It was why this particular collection of layouts and calculations appeared in material connected to the competing game. And one of the files came with an even stranger detail.

## A04 — THE 2013 TIMESTAMP

**P021**

One workbook discussed by the judge was called Inception Conception. It had been used for Sky Train, an early version of Dragon Train. The remarkable detail wasn't the workbook's name. It was the date carried in the file's history.

**P022**

The court compared the early Sky Train workbook with an older Dragon Link spreadsheet. Similarities in their structure and file history raised a question more specific than whether both games featured dragons. One date in particular stood out—and it came from years before Dragon Train was released.

**P023** **[SHORT S04 — text/legal Stage04 pass; audio timing unverified]**

A Light & Wonder development spreadsheet for an early Dragon Train version carried a creation date of November twelfth, 2013, according to a federal judge. It matched an older Aristocrat workbook's timestamp. The court treated that as a clue about origin, not a final verdict that every calculation in the rival game was copied.

**P024**

The opinion also discussed overlapping prize-assignment methods and jackpot-related calculations. To a casual player, two jackpots may look similar. To a mathematician, the precise logic behind their probabilities can tell a more complicated story. The point wasn't one familiar feature. It was how separate details fit together.

**P025**

Still, a timestamp is not a confession. And a preliminary order is not the same thing as a jury weighing the entire record at trial. The judge was deciding whether immediate protection was warranted while the case continued.

## A05 — THE DEFENSE

**P026**

Light & Wonder had an answer. Its developers, the company argued, had done independent work. Some final features differed, and some mathematical patterns could be derived from a game itself. That matters: a competitor can study a product without automatically stealing a trade secret.

**P027** **[SHORT S05 — text/legal Stage04 pass; audio timing unverified]**

A federal judge agreed with Light & Wonder on part of the Dragon Train math dispute. In a preliminary ruling, the court said certain bonus-symbol probabilities might be inferred from visible gameplay. But other mathematical combinations still raised serious trade-secret concerns. One successful defense argument didn't clear the company of the broader claim.

**P028**

Think of the difference between noticing how often a visible feature appears and opening a designer's private workbook. The first might be calculated from observation. The second can reveal choices nobody outside the development team was meant to have. The law doesn't treat those things as automatically identical.

**P029**

The judge examined that distinction rather than rejecting every argument from Light & Wonder. But on important combinations of game math and documentary similarities, the preliminary analysis favored Aristocrat. That was enough to change what could happen next.

**P030**

The word preliminary matters. The court was assessing the likelihood of success and the need for interim relief, not issuing a final verdict on every trade-secret, copyright, or business claim in the lawsuit.

## A06 — THE GAME IS HALTED

**P031**

By September 2024, the dispute had reached Light & Wonder's commercial plans. A temporary court ruling could affect where a game went next, the agreements around it, and how the manufacturer responded to customers. A lawsuit about invisible information was becoming visible in the marketplace.

**P032** **[SHORT S06 — text/legal Stage04 pass; audio timing unverified]**

A federal judge temporarily restricted Light & Wonder's Dragon Train in September 2024, while Aristocrat's trade-secret case continued. Light & Wonder disagreed with the order. When the companies settled in 2026, that preliminary injunction was vacated. But the separate settlement agreement still stopped commercialization of the affected games.

**P033**

The court also addressed machines already placed with customers and set a deadline for compliance reporting. The dispute had reached casinos and operators, not just developers and attorneys. Public records reviewed here do not establish how many machines were ultimately removed worldwide.

**P034**

Light & Wonder pushed back publicly. In a September 2024 statement filed with securities regulators, it disagreed with the ruling and discussed an appeal. It also estimated Dragon Train would account for less than five percent of a targeted earnings measure for 2025. That was a forecast, not an audited calculation of lost profit.

**P035**

And now the stakes widened. Because the next development did not concern Dragon Train alone.

## A07 — TWO GAMES, TWO COURTS

**P036**

Aristocrat was also pursuing proceedings in Australia. Two countries were dealing with related claims, but not in precisely the same way. Saying one global court banned everything would erase that difference.

**P037**

In February 2025, an Australian court refused additional mandatory interim orders that Aristocrat wanted. Light & Wonder had already offered undertakings that extended important U.S.-style restrictions into Australia. That refusal was not a ruling that no confidential information had been misused. It concerned whether more immediate measures were necessary.

**P038**

By July 2025, a stipulated U.S. order extended provisional restrictions to another Light & Wonder family, Jewel of the Dragon. The dispute now concerned two named families, not just one successful rival product.

**P039**

The case also reached questions about mathematical models in certain other Hold and Spin games. That did not establish that every product in the company's catalog violated Aristocrat's rights. It meant additional issues would need to be addressed.

**P040**

The case kept raising questions about files, formulas, games, and court orders in two countries. In early 2026, the companies agreed to resolve the direct dispute before any jury trial produced a final damages verdict.

## A08 — THE PRICE AND THE PAPER

**P041**

On January eleventh, 2026, Aristocrat and Light & Wonder announced their settlement. It covered the direct dispute and established what Light & Wonder would pay and what would happen to the affected games. The price was one hundred and twenty-seven point five million U.S. dollars.

**P042**

The language matters. Light & Wonder acknowledged that certain mathematical information had been used in developing Dragon Train and Jewel of the Dragon. The court filing described that information as material Aristocrat contended was proprietary and confidential. That is more precise than saying an entire casino game, every calculation, or every visual feature was judicially proven stolen.

In February 2026, a federal court vacated temporary injunctions in Aristocrat's case against Light & Wonder. Yet Dragon Train and Jewel of the Dragon remained subject to permanent commercial restrictions. Why? Light & Wonder had agreed to those restrictions in its settlement contract. The court orders ended; the contractual promise survived.

**P044**

The agreement covered commercialization of both families in land-based and digital form. Light & Wonder also agreed to stop using the mathematical information at issue and destroy documents reflecting it. Both companies described a best-efforts commitment to remove existing installations; the reviewed records do not establish a final worldwide removal count.

**P045**

Light & Wonder also reported roughly one hundred and twenty-eight million dollars of settlement-related expense in its 2025 results. That accounting charge relates to the same settlement. It isn't another giant payment to add on top.

**P046**

The company later reported that the direct Aristocrat claims against specified Light & Wonder entities in Australia were dismissed in March. Procedures for certain other game-math issues remained subject to the parties' agreement. The corporate IP dispute had a negotiated end; that did not make every possible consequence disappear.
