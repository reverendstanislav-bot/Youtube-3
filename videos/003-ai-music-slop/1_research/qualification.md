## Strongest hook
A North Carolina musician emails himself the math — 1,040 bot accounts, about 661,440 streams a day, $1,207,128 a year (indictment ¶14, pp.7–8, allegation) — and the only thing he runs short of is songs. So he buys them from AI by the thousand, names them "Zygotic Washstands" and "Calm Knuckles", and later pleads guilty and agrees to forfeit $8,091,843.64. His lawyers now argue no artist "suffered any perceptible harm" (reported, S013). The question the whole film answers: if the pool is shared, who paid?

## Story potential
- **Protagonist(s):** Michael Smith, a working musician in Cornelius, NC, who (per the indictment) turned his small catalog into a bot-streaming operation; secondarily the prosecutors (Williams, then Clayton) and UMG as the industry enforcer.
- **Antagonist / force:** the pro-rata royalty pool itself — a shared pot that pays by share of streams, so volume beats listeners — and the open-access distribution pipe that AI now floods (Deezer: >50% of uploads at peak).
- **The turn:** 2018 — Smith's constraint was never bots but songs; AI removed it ("we've proved the model works"). Industry turn: the fight moves from fraudsters to distributors (Believe 2024 → DistroKid 2026).
- **The cost:** $8,091,843.64 agreed forfeiture and a federal conviction for Smith; royalties held back by the MLC; an undisclosed settlement for Believe; for everyone else, a diluted pool the defense calls "a drop in an ocean."
- **Open question:** what sentence Judge Koeltl imposes for the first U.S. AI-streaming-fraud case — and whether a distributor can be held liable for the flood it carries (DistroKid, pending, denied).
