# PART 1

## S01

In October twenty seventeen, a North Carolina businessman named Michael Smith sent himself an email. According to a federal indictment, it was a business plan — written as arithmetic.

Fifty-two cloud accounts. Twenty bots on each. One thousand forty fake listeners.

At half a cent a stream, the email projected one million, two hundred seven thousand, one hundred twenty-eight dollars a year.

That email comes from the indictment. It's an allegation. This isn't: in March twenty twenty-six, Smith pleaded guilty to one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud — and agreed to a money judgment of eight million, ninety-one thousand, eight hundred forty-three dollars and sixty-four cents.

He did the math. This is what it cost.

## S02

This isn't really a story about bots. It's about a pool of money that every artist on a streaming service shares — and about a new kind of song that, prosecutors say, let one man draw from that pool for years.

According to the indictment, the songs he needed weren't written by a person. They came from an AI music company, under a contract for up to ten thousand songs a month.

And at the center of this case sits a question nobody has answered. Spotify says its protections held what Smith got from Spotify to a small fraction of the total. So who paid the rest? The public record doesn't say.

On Tuesday, October sixth, a federal judge is set to decide what the rest of this costs him. His lawyers argue, according to Billboard, that each fake stream's effect was "a drop in an ocean." The prosecutor who announced his guilty plea put it differently: "the millions of dollars Smith stole was real."

## S03

Before any of this, there was a real music career. A small one.

Rolling Stone reported that Smith owned a chain of medical clinics, co-founded a record label, and bankrolled a hip-hop competition on B-E-T called One Shot — where he also sat as a judge. He called it "the first nationally-televised competition specific to hip-hop."

"That's the beauty of monitored. They can't manipulate it."

That's what a Nashville radio promoter told Rolling Stone about a twenty seventeen single by Michael Smith — "You're My Kind of Beautiful." Monitored charts are built on real stations reporting real spins. The song peaked at number thirty-five on Billboard's adult-contemporary chart.

The next year, an album Smith made with a collaborator reached number one on Billboard's jazz charts. He posted: "I officially have a Number One Billboard Album!"

The federal case against him isn't about those charts. It's about streaming royalties. In October twenty seventeen, according to a federal indictment, Smith emailed himself a plan for one thousand forty bot accounts.

He later pleaded guilty to one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud.

## S04

To see why that plan could pay, you need one idea.

Streaming services don't pay a fixed price per play. They put a share of their revenue into a pool. Then the pool is split by each rightsholder's share of all the streams.

Picture a simplified pot: one hundred dollars, and one hundred streams. Each stream is worth a dollar. Add one hundred fake streams. The pot doesn't grow — every stream is now worth fifty cents. The artists behind the one hundred real streams now split fifty dollars instead of one hundred. That's our illustration, not a number from the case.

So a fake stream doesn't create new money. In the prosecutors' telling, it takes a slice of the same pot — money that would otherwise go to artists that real people chose to hear.

Hold on to that pot. By the end, the hardest question in this case is who actually lost money from it.

# PART 2

## S05

By May twenty seventeen, according to the indictment, Smith was emailing an associate: "Make up names and addresses."

He bought fake email accounts in bulk, the indictment says, and registered bot accounts under made-up names — often on family plans, because they cost less per user. The bots, prosecutors say, ran in browser tabs on rented cloud computers, with modified macros looping the music, and V-P-Ns hiding that it all ran from his home. The indictment says he told his helpers the setup had to be "undetectable."

But the machine had a weak point. It wasn't the bots. It was the songs.

Put a billion streams on one track, and someone notices. Spread them across tens of thousands of tracks, and each one is played only a few times. That, prosecutors say, is why he needed more music.

In May twenty nineteen, according to the indictment, he put it plainly: "If we get too many streams on one song it comes down."

## S06

Then the warnings started.

"I acknowledge what you wrote and I ask that you still release it." Prosecutors say Michael Smith wrote that in March twenty eighteen, after a music distribution company warned him that one of his songs "fit the criteria that is usually linked to store end streaming abuse."

He added: "We have no intentions of committing streaming fraud."

That October, after what the company called "multiple reports of streaming abuse from different stores," he replied: "This is absolutely wrong and crazy! There is absolutely no fraud going on whatsoever! How can I appeal this?"

In March twenty nineteen, he told a streaming platform: "You have slandered me to my distributors."

Every one of those lines is written into the federal charge Smith later pleaded guilty to — conspiracy to commit wire fraud. That charge calls his twenty nineteen statements false.

## S07

Smith's own catalog, the indictment says, was "not nearly large enough." He tried a publicist's catalog. He even tried selling fake streams as a service.

On October fourth, twenty eighteen, according to the indictment, he wrote to two associates: "in order to not raise any issues with the powers that be we need a ton of content with small amounts of streams."

The answer was already arriving. According to the indictment, that year Smith began working with the chief executive of an AI music company, and with a music promoter.

By October eighteenth, Smith was reporting hundreds of thousands of streams on AI-made tracks. The promoter, who had called it a "proof of concept," wrote back: "we've proved the model works."

Up to ten thousand songs a month. According to a federal indictment, that's what Michael Smith contracted for in February twenty nineteen — with the chief executive of an AI music company.

It was called a Master Services Agreement. Under it, the indictment says, Smith would own the songs outright and supply the song and artist names. The AI company would get two thousand dollars a month or fifteen percent of the streaming revenue, whichever was greater.

The files arrived named like random code. Smith, prosecutors say, gave them fake song and artist names. Songs called "Zygophyceae." "Zygotic Washstands." "Zyme Bedewing." Artists called "Calliope Bloom," "Calm Knuckles," "Calorie Screams" — and "Camel Edible."

A month later, the indictment says, the AI executive wrote to him: "this is not 'music,' it's 'instant music.'"

The executive isn't named in court papers, and neither is the promoter who worked with them. Neither has been publicly charged. Smith has pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud.

On June fifth, twenty nineteen, according to the indictment, Smith wrote: "We are at eighty-eight million total streams so far!" He told his two partners the streams were earning about one hundred ten thousand dollars a month, that each of them was getting ten percent — and he asked for another ten thousand songs, to "spread this out more."

# PART 3

## S08

The next years ran on the same engine, only bigger.

August twenty twenty, the AI executive, per the indictment: "Song quality is ten X to twenty X better now, and we also have vocal generation capabilities."

At certain points, prosecutors allege, Smith had as many as ten thousand active bot accounts.

Those accounts had to be paid for. From about twenty twenty to twenty twenty-three, the indictment alleges, Smith moved one point three million dollars of royalties through an account called S-M-H Entertainment into a Manhattan debit-card service — using dozens of fake employee names — to pay for bot accounts.

That became a money-laundering charge. It is not part of the charge he pleaded guilty to.

## S09

"Mike is the 'human' author!" That's what Michael Smith's representatives wrote in March twenty twenty-three, according to a federal indictment, after the songwriters' royalty collective, the M-L-C, stopped paying him.

In the same exchange, they wrote: "Mike categorically denies any play manipulation."

According to the same indictment, an AI company had been supplying him songs under contract since twenty nineteen.

When Smith was arrested the next year, the M-L-C's chief executive said it had "withheld payment of the associated mechanical royalties." The M-L-C hasn't said how much it held back.

Smith later pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud; the rest is the indictment's account.

In February twenty twenty-four — less than a year after the M-L-C stopped paying him — Smith wrote, per the indictment, that his music had "generated at this point over four billion streams and twelve million dollars in royalties since twenty nineteen."

That's his own number, as prosecutors quote it. Not a finding. Remember it — the numbers in this case keep changing.

## S10

On the morning of Wednesday, September fourth, twenty twenty-four, Smith was arrested at his home outside Charlotte.

The same day, the Justice Department announced what it called the "First Criminal Case Involving Artificially Inflated Music Streaming."

Three counts: wire fraud conspiracy, wire fraud, and money-laundering conspiracy. Each carried up to twenty years in prison. Prosecutors alleged he had obtained more than ten million dollars in royalties.

The U.S. Attorney at the time said: "it's time for Smith to face the music."

Smith's lawyer declined to comment. He was released on a five hundred thousand dollar personal recognizance bond.

The case headed toward trial. In early twenty twenty-six, Rolling Stone reported it was expected to start on October sixth.

# PART 4

## S11

There was no trial.

Three federal counts. Up to twenty years each. Michael Smith, indicted in twenty twenty-four, never pleaded guilty to any of them.

Instead, on March nineteenth, twenty twenty-six, prosecutors filed a brand-new, one-count charge: conspiracy to commit wire fraud, under a different statute — section three seventy-one. Smith gave up his right to an indictment and was charged on it that same day.

Same kind of crime. A different law — with a much lower ceiling.

He pleaded guilty to it before U.S. District Judge John Koeltl, under a plea agreement. The agreement itself isn't public.

The maximum he faces on it: five years, not twenty.

It is a federal conviction. And it came with a price: a money judgment of eight million, ninety-one thousand, eight hundred forty-three dollars and sixty-four cents.

As of late September twenty twenty-six, he had not been sentenced.

There's a sentence in the forfeiture order Michael Smith agreed to after his guilty plea that sounds like a confession that millions of dollars vanished. It isn't.

The order sets his money judgment — eight million dollars and change — as the proceeds he personally obtained. And in it, he admits those proceeds "cannot be located upon the exercise of due diligence."

It sounds dramatic. It's actually a legal formula — the trigger words from the federal forfeiture statute. The same words were already in his twenty twenty-four indictment.

What they do is practical. They let the government go after his other property instead, up to whatever remains unpaid.

So the eight million dollars is a debt the court has entered against him — final as to him, and set to become part of his sentence.

No public document we found says whether any of it has been collected.

Now line the numbers up. His own email, as the indictment quotes it, claimed twelve million dollars. The indictment alleged more than ten million dollars. The judgment: just over eight million dollars. Why the figure fell from ten to eight, the public record we reviewed doesn't explain.

Which leaves the question this whole case keeps circling. Eight million dollars in royalties, by the Justice Department's count. Whose money was it?

## S12

Spotify says Michael Smith got only about sixty thousand dollars from Spotify. He has pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and agreed to a money judgment of more than eight million dollars.

So who paid him?

Spotify's answer came eight days after his arrest. Its measures, it said, "limited the royalties Smith was able to game from Spotify to approximately sixty thousand dollars of the ten million dollars noted in the indictment." Spotify says it usually accounts for about half of all streams in the market.

Pandora put its estimated royalty exposure at fifteen hundred dollars.

The songwriters' collective, the M-L-C, says it caught him early and withheld payment.

When Digital Music News asked Apple Music, Boomplay, Tidal, YouTube and Amazon Music what they paid him, none of them responded. SoundCloud wouldn't give a figure. Not answering isn't an answer, either way.

These are the companies' own accounts. Which services paid most of the money has never been publicly established.

Michael Lewan, who runs an industry anti-fraud group, the Music Fights Fraud Alliance, was speaking about streaming fraud industry-wide, not about Smith's case, when he told Rolling Stone: "If you view it as one or five or ten percent — that is a percentage of royalties not going to artists, creators, and songwriters." He also said he hadn't seen a "trusted percentage or number" for how big streaming fraud really is.

The head of the recording industry's global body, I-F-P-I, puts it more bluntly: "This is theft, plain and simple."

Money that, prosecutors say, came out of real artists' share — spread so thin it's hard to point to any one artist's slice. That gap is where Smith's lawyers built their case for mercy.

# PART 5

## S13

On September twenty-second, twenty twenty-six, Smith's lawyers filed their sentencing memo.

Michael Smith's lawyers have a metaphor for the fraud conspiracy he pleaded guilty to.

According to Billboard — we haven't seen the memo ourselves — their sentencing memo says: "The effect each individual stream of one artist's song has on every other artist's share of total streams is not akin to a drop in a proverbial bucket — it is a drop in an ocean."

And: "No individual artist or songwriter suffered any perceptible harm from Mister Smith's conduct."

They're asking for probation.

Billboard reports the sentencing guidelines call for forty-six to fifty-seven months, and the Probation Office recommended twenty-four. The maximum is five years.

In May, his lawyers questioned "whether the section two B one point one guidelines fairly address Mister Smith's culpability." That's the fraud guideline — and it scales with loss. If the loss is spread across an ocean, how do you measure it?

Sentencing was set for October sixth, twenty twenty-six. That's when Judge Koeltl decides how much a drop in an ocean costs.

The memo goes further. Per Billboard, it argues that bot-driven fraud is "indisputably rampant... including by big labels and publishers." That is the defense's claim.

It points to no criminal record and to letters of support: "While he made serious mistakes here, those mistakes do not define him as a person."

A rapper who worked with Smith on One Shot, Kxng Crooked, told Rolling Stone: "Whatever he did, he made that bed and he's got to lie in it." But, he added: "Let's not take the man who's the independent guy and string him up."

# PART 6

## S14

In twenty eighteen, according to the indictment, Smith's problem was that he couldn't get enough songs.

At its June twenty twenty-six peak, one streaming service, Deezer, says it was receiving nearly ninety thousand fully AI-generated tracks a day — more than half of everything uploaded.

Deezer says AI music makes up only one to three percent of streams on Deezer, but that up to eighty-five percent of those streams in twenty twenty-five were fraudulent, and were demonetized. And it says the impact on human artists' royalties is "minimal on Deezer" — because it strips those streams out of the pool.

On September fourteenth, twenty twenty-six, the recording-industry body I-F-P-I launched a "Streaming Integrity Initiative," asking distributors to verify their customers and vet uploads for AI-related risks. Universal, Sony and Warner signed on.

The industry has also gone to court — against distributors, the companies that deliver music to the streaming services. In twenty twenty-four, U-M-G and other music companies sued one of them, Believe, and its TuneCore service — seeking at least five hundred million dollars over uploads under names like "Kendrik Laamar" and "Jutin Biber." Believe said it "strongly refuted" the claims. In April twenty twenty-six the case was dismissed after a settlement. Terms weren't disclosed; no admission of wrongdoing has been made public.

The newest fight is a separate one — a civil lawsuit about uploads and copyright. It is not part of Smith's criminal case.

Four thousand, five hundred sixty-two tracks in twelve months — from one account. That's what Universal Music Group alleges about the busiest account on the distributor DistroKid, in a lawsuit filed September fifteenth, twenty twenty-six, in federal court in Delaware.

The suit claims deceptive trade practices and copyright infringement. At two other high-volume accounts, the complaint alleges, over ninety-seven and over ninety-eight percent of the tracks are raw outputs from the AI generator Suno. And it alleges DistroKid accounts for more than half of weekly track releases on one major service — nearly twelve million tracks in six months.

U-M-G also draws a line: "This lawsuit is not about the distribution of AI-generated music when clearly disclosed as such."

DistroKid's response: "We strongly disagree with U-M-G's allegations... We are confident in our practices and intend to defend DistroKid vigorously."

These are allegations in a civil case that has only just been filed. Nothing has been decided.

## S15

As this film is released, at the end of September twenty twenty-six, Michael Smith is due back before Judge Koeltl on Tuesday, October sixth, at three p.m. It's the same date Rolling Stone once reported for his trial. Music Business Worldwide describes it as the first sentence in the U.S. for AI-assisted streaming fraud.

Here is what will decide it.

Whether the judge accepts that a drop in an ocean caused no perceptible harm. Where he lands between probation, which the defense wants; the twenty-four months the Probation Office recommended, according to Billboard; and the forty-six-to-fifty-seven-month guideline range Billboard reported — all under a five-year cap.
