## S04
<!-- Mechanism, minimum dose: the pro-rata pool, with a simplified pot; why a fake stream takes from real artists (prosecution framing) -->

To see why that plan could pay, you need one idea.

Streaming services don't pay a fixed price per play. They put a share of their revenue into a pool. Then the pool is split by each rightsholder's share of all the streams. {C044}

Picture a simplified pot: $100, and 100 streams. Each stream is worth a dollar. Add 100 fake streams. The pot doesn't grow — every stream is now worth fifty cents. The artists behind the 100 real streams now split $50 instead of $100. That's our illustration, not a number from the case.

So a fake stream doesn't create new money. In the prosecutors' telling, it takes a slice of the same pot — money that would otherwise go to artists that real people chose to hear. {C044}

Hold on to that pot. By the end, the hardest question in this case is who actually lost money from it.

## S05

[SH07>]
There's a sentence in the forfeiture order Michael Smith agreed to after his guilty plea {C001} that sounds like a confession that millions of dollars vanished. It isn't.

The order sets his money judgment — $8 million and change — as the proceeds he personally obtained. {C005} And in it, he admits those proceeds "cannot be located upon the exercise of due diligence." {C006}

What they do is practical. They let the government go after his other property instead, up to whatever remains unpaid. {C007}

So the $8 million is a debt the court has entered against him — final as to him, and set to become part of his sentence. {C008}
